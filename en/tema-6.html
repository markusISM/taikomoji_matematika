The concept of the derivative

1

Average rate of change and the secant line

A function describes how one quantity depends on another. When the input changes from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), i.e. by \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1\), the value of the function changes by \(\Delta y = f(x_2) - f(x_1)\). The quotient

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} &= \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} \\ &= \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \end{aligned} \]

is the average rate of change over that interval. Geometrically, it is the slope of the secant line joining the two corresponding points on the graph.

The secant line is a straight line, so its slope is calculated in the same way as in Topic 1: from two points on the line.

What the slope of the secant line means in practice

Suppose that a household’s monthly consumption expenditure \(C\) (in euros) depends on its monthly disposable income \(x\) (in euros), for incomes from 0 to 4000 euros, as follows:

\[ C(x) = 100 + 0.9x - 0.0001x^2 \]

When income is 1000 euros, consumption expenditure is \(C(1000) = 100 + 900 - 100 = 900\) euros; when income is 3000 euros, it is \(C(3000) = 100 + 2700 - 900 = 1900\) euros. On the graph, these are the points \(A(1000;\, 900)\) and \(B(3000;\, 1900)\).

The slope of the secant line:

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{1900 - 900}{3000 - 1000} = \frac{1000}{2000} = 0.5 \]

Interpretation. When disposable income rises from 1000 to 3000 euros, each additional euro of income increases consumption expenditure by 0.50 euro on average, and the remaining 0.50 euro is saved. The slope of the secant line is measured in euros of consumption expenditure per euro of income.

This is an average for the whole 2000-euro increase in income. The graph is not a straight line: it is steeper at lower incomes and flatter at higher incomes. Therefore the slope of the secant line does not show how consumption expenditure responds to a change in income when income is exactly 1000 euros. This question is answered by the derivative.

2

The derivative – the instantaneous rate of change

What is the slope of the graph at a single point? Let us return to the consumption example. Suppose that the household’s disposable income is 1000 euros. By how much will consumption expenditure increase if income rises by one more euro? The secant slope of 0.5 does not answer this question, because it describes the whole interval from 1000 to 3000 euros. We need the slope of the graph at the point \(A\) itself.

This slope can be found by moving the second point of the secant line towards the first, i.e. by letting the change in the input \(\Delta x\) approach zero. The point \(B\) then moves along the graph towards \(A\), and the secant line differs less and less from the tangent line – the line that passes through \(A\) and shows the direction of the graph at that point.

The definition of the derivative

As \(\Delta x \to 0\), the second point approaches the first. If the slope of the secant line \(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\) approaches a finite limit, this limit gives the slope of the tangent line. It is the derivative of the function at \(x\):

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} \]

This definition connects two interpretations: the derivative is an instantaneous rate of change and the slope of a tangent line.

Finding a derivative is called differentiation. If the finite limit exists at every point of an open interval, the function is differentiable on that interval. Derivatives can be calculated from the definition, but the rules below usually make the calculation simpler. These rules follow from the definition.

What the derivative shows in the consumption example

The table showed that the slope of the tangent line at \(A\) equals 0.7, i.e. \(C'(1000) = 0.7\). This means that when disposable income is 1000 euros, an additional euro of income increases consumption expenditure by approximately 0.70 euro, and about 0.30 euro is saved. In economics, this quantity is called the marginal propensity to consume.

The derivative is itself a function, so it can be calculated for any level of income. By the rules below, \(C'(x) = 0.9 - 0.0002x\) (see card 7). When income is 3000 euros, \(C'(3000) = 0.3\): an additional euro of income increases consumption expenditure by only about 0.30 euro. In this model, as income grows, an ever smaller part of each additional euro is spent on consumption, so the graph becomes flatter.

Secant line or tangent line? The slope of the secant line, 0.5, is the average rate of change over the interval \([1000,\, 3000]\). The slope of the tangent line, \(C'(1000) = 0.7\), is the instantaneous rate of change at \(x = 1000\).

3

Two interpretations of the derivative and notation

The following two interpretations describe the same derivative from different perspectives:

  • As a rate of change. \(f'(x)\) measures the instantaneous change in \(f(x)\) per unit change in \(x\). To estimate the change in the function, multiply the derivative by a small change in the input: \(\Delta y \approx f'(x)\cdot \Delta x\). For example, if a disposable income of 1000 euros rises by 20 euros, consumption expenditure increases by approximately \(0.7\cdot 20 = 14\) euros.
  • As the slope of the tangent line. \(f'(x_1)\) is the slope of the tangent to the graph at \(x = x_1\). A positive derivative indicates an upward-sloping tangent; a negative derivative indicates a downward-sloping tangent.

Notation

The derivative of \(f(x)\) is written \(f'(x)\) throughout this topic. The notations \(y'\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}f(x)\) are also used. The derivative at a particular point is written, for example, as \(f'(5)\). How to calculate it is shown in card 7.

The derivatives of the elementary functions

4

Table of derivatives

This table lists the basic derivatives used throughout the topic. Apply each formula where the derivative exists; trigonometric arguments are measured in radians.

1Constants and powers
  • \(c' = 0\)
  • \((x^n)' = nx^{n-1}\)
  • \(x' = 1\)
  • \((\sqrt{x})' = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\)
2Exponential and logarithmic functions
  • \((a^x)' = a^x \ln a\)
  • \((e^x)' = e^x\)
  • \((\log_a x)' = \dfrac{1}{x \ln a}\)
  • \((\ln x)' = \dfrac{1}{x}\)
3Trigonometric functions
  • \((\sin x)' = \cos x\)
  • \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\)
  • \((\tan x)' = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x}\)
  • \((\cot x)' = -\dfrac{1}{\sin^2 x}\)

Roots and reciprocals as powers

Roots and reciprocals can be written as powers. This allows us to use the power rule in both cases:

\[ \begin{gathered} \sqrt{x} = x^{0.5} \\ \Longrightarrow\; (\sqrt{x})' = 0.5x^{-0.5} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{1}{x} = x^{-1} \\ \Longrightarrow\; \left(\frac{1}{x}\right)' = -x^{-2} = -\frac{1}{x^2} \end{gathered} \]

More generally, \(\dfrac{1}{x^n} = x^{-n}\), so

\[ \left(\frac{1}{x^n}\right)' = -nx^{-n-1} = -\frac{n}{x^{n+1}} \]

The rules of differentiation

5

The constant multiple rule

\[ \big(c\, u(x)\big)' = c\, u'(x) \]

When it applies. A constant multiplies a function. Keep the constant unchanged and multiply it by the derivative of the function:

\[ \begin{gathered} (6x^5)' = 6\cdot 5x^4 = 30x^4 \\ (5e^x)' = 5e^x \end{gathered} \]
6

The sum and difference rule

\[ \big(u(x) \pm v(x)\big)' = u'(x) \pm v'(x) \]

When it applies. Terms are joined by addition or subtraction. Differentiate each term separately and retain its sign:

\[ (\sqrt{x} + \sin x)' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} + \cos x \]

Use this rule together with the constant multiple rule to differentiate polynomials and sums of the elementary functions in the table.

7

The value of the derivative at a particular point

The notation \(f'(a)\) means the value of the derivative at \(x = a\). Find it in two steps, in exactly this order:

  1. first find the derivative as a function, \(f'(x)\);
  2. then substitute the specified input value \(x = a\) into the derivative.

Example 1

Find \(C'(1000)\) if \(C(x) = 100 + 0.9x - 0.0001x^2\).

Step 1 – the derivative as a function. Differentiate each term (the sum and the constant multiple rules). The derivative of the constant 100 is zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} C'(x) &= 0 + 0.9 - 0.0001\cdot 2x \\ &= 0.9 - 0.0002x \end{aligned} \]

Step 2 – the value at the point. Substitute \(x = 1000\) into the derivative:

\[ C'(1000) = 0.9 - 0.0002\cdot 1000 = 0.7 \]

This is the same slope of the tangent line that we found in card 2 by decreasing \(\Delta x\).

Example 2

Find \(f'(4)\) if \(f(x) = x^3 - 6\sqrt{x}\).

Step 1 – the derivative as a function.

\[ f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} = 3x^2 - \frac{3}{\sqrt{x}} \]

Step 2 – the value at the point. Substitute \(x = 4\) into the derivative:

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(4) &= 3\cdot 16 - \frac{3}{\sqrt{4}} \\ &= 48 - 1.5 = 46.5 \end{aligned} \]

Common mistake – swapping the steps. If you first substitute \(x = 1000\) into the function and only then differentiate, you get the number \(C(1000) = 900\), and the derivative of any number is zero: \((900)' = 0\). This zero is not \(C'(1000)\). Also, do not confuse two different quantities: \(C(1000) = 900\) is consumption expenditure in euros, while \(C'(1000) = 0.7\) is its rate of change in euros per euro of income.

8

The product rule

\[ \big(u(x)\cdot v(x)\big)' = u'(x)\cdot v(x) + u(x)\cdot v'(x) \]

When it applies. Two functions of \(x\) are multiplied, as in \(x^2 \ln x\) or \(xe^{-3x}\). The same rule applies to other variables, as in \(\sqrt{t}\cdot e^{-2t}\).

Which factor comes first. Either factor may be labelled \(u(x)\); the result is the same. The derivative has two terms: differentiate the first factor and keep the second unchanged, then add the first factor multiplied by the derivative of the second.

Example

Find the derivative of the function \(f(x) = 5x^3e^x\).

Why the product rule? The function is the product of two functions of \(x\): \(5x^3\) and \(e^x\). Both factors change with \(x\), so the product rule applies.

The functions \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\). Label the first factor \(u(x)\) and the second \(v(x)\), and find their derivatives:

  • First factor \(u(x) = 5x^3\), \(u'(x) = 15x^2\)
  • Second factor \(v(x) = e^x\), \(v'(x) = e^x\)

The formula. Substitute these four expressions into the formula \(u'(x)\cdot v(x) + u(x)\cdot v'(x)\):

\[ f'(x) = 15x^2\cdot e^x + 5x^3\cdot e^x \]

Simplifying. Both terms share the factor \(5x^2e^x\). Factoring it out gives:

\[ f'(x) = 5x^2e^x(3 + x) \]

Common mistake. The expression \(u'(x)\cdot v'(x)\) is not the product rule: multiplying the two derivatives omits the required terms.

9

The quotient rule

\[ \left(\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}\right)' = \frac{u'(x)v(x) - u(x)v'(x)}{v^2(x)} \]

When it applies. A fraction has a denominator that depends on \(x\). Label the numerator \(u(x)\) and the denominator \(v(x)\). In the derivative, square the original denominator. The rule applies where the denominator is nonzero. A constant denominator can instead be treated as a constant multiple: \(\dfrac{u(x)}{5} = \dfrac{1}{5}u(x)\).

The order of the two products. Start the numerator with \(u'(x)v(x)\), then subtract \(u(x)v'(x)\). The order matters: reversing the two products changes the sign of the result. Keep brackets around each product until you have expanded and simplified the numerator.

Example

Find the derivative of the function \(f(x) = \dfrac{8x}{1 + \ln x}\).

Why the quotient rule? The function is the quotient of two functions of \(x\), and its denominator \(1 + \ln x\) depends on \(x\). Therefore the quotient rule applies.

The functions \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\). Label the numerator \(u(x)\) and the denominator \(v(x)\). Unlike in a product, these two functions cannot be swapped.

  • Numerator \(u(x) = 8x\), \(u'(x) = 8\)
  • Denominator \(v(x) = 1 + \ln x\), \(v'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\)

The formula. Substitute these expressions into the formula; in the numerator, first write \(u'(x)v(x)\), then subtract \(u(x)v'(x)\):

\[ f'(x) = \frac{8\cdot(1 + \ln x) - 8x\cdot\frac{1}{x}}{(1 + \ln x)^2} \]

Simplifying. Since \(8x\cdot\frac{1}{x} = 8\), the numerator equals \(8 + 8\ln x - 8 = 8\ln x\):

\[ f'(x) = \frac{8\ln x}{(1 + \ln x)^2} \]
10

The chain rule

\[ \big(g(u(x))\big)' = g'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) \]

When it applies. One function is applied to another expression, for example a power, root, exponential, \(\ln\), \(\sin\) or \(\cos\). The outer function \(g(u)\) is the operation performed last when evaluating the function at \(x\); the inner function \(u(x)\) is its input.

How to apply it. Find the derivative of a composite function in three steps:

  1. identify the outer function \(g(u)\) and the inner function \(u(x)\);
  2. find the derivatives of both functions, \(g'(u)\) and \(u'(x)\);
  3. in \(g'(u)\), replace \(u\) with the whole inner function \(u(x)\) and multiply by \(u'(x)\).

Example

Find the derivative of the function \(f(x) = e^{2x - 1}\).

To evaluate \(e^{2x - 1}\), first calculate the exponent \(2x - 1\), then raise \(e\) to that result. Thus:

  • Outer \(g(u) = e^u\), \(g'(u) = e^u\)
  • Inner \(u(x) = 2x - 1\), \(u'(x) = 2\)

In \(g'(u) = e^u\), replace \(u\) with \(2x - 1\) and multiply by \(u'(x) = 2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= g'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) \\ &= e^{2x - 1}\cdot 2 = 2e^{2x - 1} \end{aligned} \]

For the functions in the table, this gives the following formulas. Each pair of formulas is followed by two examples.

1Power and root
  • \(\big((u(x))^n\big)' = n(u(x))^{n-1}\cdot u'(x)\)
  • \(\left(\sqrt{u(x)}\right)' = \dfrac{u'(x)}{2\sqrt{u(x)}}\)

Find \(f'(x)\) if \(f(x) = (x^2 + 3x)^5\).

  • Outer \(g(u) = u^5\), \(g'(u) = 5u^4\)
  • Inner \(u(x) = x^2 + 3x\), \(u'(x) = 2x + 3\)
\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= g'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) \\ &= 5(x^2 + 3x)^4\cdot(2x + 3) \end{aligned} \]

Find \(f'(x)\) if \(f(x) = \sqrt{x^3 + 1}\).

  • Outer \(g(u) = \sqrt{u}\), \(g'(u) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\)
  • Inner \(u(x) = x^3 + 1\), \(u'(x) = 3x^2\)
\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= g'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) \\ &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^3 + 1}}\cdot 3x^2 \\ &= \frac{3x^2}{2\sqrt{x^3 + 1}} \end{aligned} \]
2Exponential and logarithmic functions
  • \(\left(e^{u(x)}\right)' = e^{u(x)}\cdot u'(x)\)
  • \(\big(\ln u(x)\big)' = \dfrac{u'(x)}{u(x)}\)

Find \(f'(x)\) if \(f(x) = e^{x^2 + 5x}\).

  • Outer \(g(u) = e^u\), \(g'(u) = e^u\)
  • Inner \(u(x) = x^2 + 5x\), \(u'(x) = 2x + 5\)
\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= g'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) \\ &= e^{x^2 + 5x}\cdot(2x + 5) \end{aligned} \]

Find \(f'(x)\) if \(f(x) = \ln(5x - 2)\).

  • Outer \(g(u) = \ln u\), \(g'(u) = \dfrac{1}{u}\)
  • Inner \(u(x) = 5x - 2\), \(u'(x) = 5\)
\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= g'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) \\ &= \frac{1}{5x - 2}\cdot 5 = \frac{5}{5x - 2} \end{aligned} \]
3Sine and cosine
  • \(\big(\sin u(x)\big)' = \cos u(x)\cdot u'(x)\)
  • \(\big(\cos u(x)\big)' = -\sin u(x)\cdot u'(x)\)

Find \(f'(x)\) if \(f(x) = \sin(4x)\).

  • Outer \(g(u) = \sin u\), \(g'(u) = \cos u\)
  • Inner \(u(x) = 4x\), \(u'(x) = 4\)
\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= g'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) \\ &= \cos(4x)\cdot 4 = 4\cos(4x) \end{aligned} \]

Find \(f'(x)\) if \(f(x) = \cos(x^2 + 1)\).

  • Outer \(g(u) = \cos u\), \(g'(u) = -\sin u\)
  • Inner \(u(x) = x^2 + 1\), \(u'(x) = 2x\)
\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= g'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) \\ &= -\sin(x^2 + 1)\cdot 2x \\ &= -2x\sin(x^2 + 1) \end{aligned} \]

Remember the inner derivative. Each formula includes \(u'(x)\), either as a factor or in the numerator of a fraction. Omitting it is a common error. Check the calculation in two steps: differentiate the outer function at its original input, then multiply by the derivative of that input. When the inner function is \(x\), its derivative is one, so the formula reduces to the basic derivative of a function of \(x\).
