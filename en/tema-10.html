The concept of the indefinite integral

1

The antiderivative

By differentiating we find how fast a function changes. By integrating we solve the opposite problem: we know the derivative, i.e. how fast a quantity changes, and look for the function that describes this quantity.

A function \(F(x)\) is called an antiderivative of \(f(x)\) on an interval \(I\) if for every \(x\) in this interval \(F'(x) = f(x)\).

In other words, an antiderivative is a function whose derivative is the given function. Therefore we check by differentiation whether a function is an antiderivative.

Example

Show that the function \(F(x) = x^3 - 2x + 5\) is an antiderivative of \(f(x) = 3x^2 - 2\).

The derivative of \(F\) is calculated by the rules of Topic 6:

\[ \begin{aligned} F'(x) &= \left(x^3 - 2x + 5\right)' \\ &= 3x^2 - 2 = f(x) \end{aligned} \]

So \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\).

The functions \(x^3 - 2x\) and \(x^3 - 2x - 7\) are antiderivatives of \(f\) as well, because the derivative of a constant is zero. So a function has not just one antiderivative but infinitely many.

2

The family of antiderivatives and integral curves

If \(G(x)\) is an antiderivative of a function \(f(x)\), then any other antiderivative \(F(x)\) of it on the same interval differs from \(G(x)\) only by a constant:

\[ F(x) = G(x) + C \]

Here \(C\) is any number. All these functions form the family of antiderivatives of \(f(x)\).

The graphs of the antiderivatives are called integral curves. Since the antiderivatives differ only by a constant, all integral curves have the same shape: each of them is obtained by shifting another curve vertically. At the same value of \(x\) all their tangent lines have the same slope, equal to \(f(x)\), because \(\big(G(x) + C\big)' = f(x)\). Therefore at such points the tangent lines are parallel.

For example, the antiderivatives of \(f(x) = 2x\) are \(F(x) = x^2 + C\), because \(\left(x^2 + C\right)' = 2x\). The graph shows several curves of this family.

3

The particular antiderivative

In problems we usually need not the whole family of antiderivatives but one particular function of it. It is chosen by an additional condition – a known value of the function at one point, \(F(x_0) = y_0\). Geometrically, this means that among all the integral curves we look for the one that passes through the point \((x_0;\, y_0)\).

The antiderivative whose graph passes through a given point \((x_0;\, y_0)\) is called the particular antiderivative.

It is found in three steps:

  1. find the whole family of antiderivatives \(F(x) = G(x) + C\);
  2. substitute \(x = x_0\) and set the value of the function equal to \(y_0\): \(G(x_0) + C = y_0\);
  3. find \(C\) from this equation and substitute it into \(F(x)\).

Example

Find the antiderivative of \(f(x) = 2x\) whose graph passes through the point \((1;\, 3)\).

The family of antiderivatives: \(F(x) = x^2 + C\). We substitute \(x = 1\) and set the value of the function equal to 3:

\[ F(1) = 1^2 + C = 3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C = 2 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F(1) = 1^2 + C = 3 \\ \Longrightarrow\quad C = 2 \end{gathered} \]

The particular antiderivative: \(F(x) = x^2 + 2\).

In applied problems this condition is usually stated in words. For example, “the fixed costs are 120 euros” means that \(TC(0) = 120\), and “the club currently has 200 members” means that \(N(0) = 200\). Such a condition is called the initial condition. It is used in both applications sections of this topic.

4

The indefinite integral

The process of finding all antiderivatives of a function is called integration. The general form of the antiderivative, i.e. the whole family of antiderivatives, is called the indefinite integral and is written as follows:

\[ \int f(x)\, dx = F(x) + C \]
  • \(\displaystyle\int\) is the integral sign;
  • \(f(x)\) is the integrand;
  • \(dx\) shows that the variable of integration is \(x\);
  • \(C\) is the integration constant. It must always be written, because the indefinite integral stands for all antiderivatives of the function.

If the variable of the function is denoted by another letter, for example time \(t\), the integral is written \(\int f(t)\, dt\).

Integration and differentiation are opposite operations

The derivative of an indefinite integral is the integrand:

\[ \begin{aligned} \left(\int f(x)\, dx\right)' &= \big(F(x) + C\big)' \\ &= f(x) \end{aligned} \]

Therefore the result of integration can always be checked: differentiate the antiderivative obtained, and the integrand must come back.

5

Properties of the indefinite integral

\[ \begin{gathered} \int k f(x)\, dx = k\int f(x)\, dx \\[4pt] \int \big(f(x) \pm g(x)\big)\, dx = \int f(x)\, dx \pm \int g(x)\, dx \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \int k f(x)\, dx = k\int f(x)\, dx \\[6pt] \begin{aligned} &\int \big(f(x) \pm g(x)\big)\, dx \\ &= \int f(x)\, dx \pm \int g(x)\, dx \end{aligned} \end{gathered} \]

A constant factor can be taken out of the integral, and the terms of a sum or a difference can be integrated separately. These properties correspond to the constant multiple and the sum and difference rules of Topic 6.

There are no such rules for a product or a quotient. The integral of a product is in general not the product of the integrals of the factors, and the integral of a quotient is not the quotient of the integrals:

\[ \begin{gathered} \int f(x)g(x)\, dx \ne \int f(x)\, dx \cdot \int g(x)\, dx \\[4pt] \int \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\, dx \ne \frac{\int f(x)\, dx}{\int g(x)\, dx} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \begin{aligned} &\int f(x)g(x)\, dx \\ &\ne \int f(x)\, dx \cdot \int g(x)\, dx \end{aligned} \\[8pt] \int \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\, dx \ne \frac{\int f(x)\, dx}{\int g(x)\, dx} \end{gathered} \]

For example, \(\int x\cdot x\, dx = \int x^2\, dx = \dfrac{x^3}{3} + C\), while the product of the integrals \(\int x\, dx\) without the constants would be \(\dfrac{x^2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{x^2}{2} = \dfrac{x^4}{4}\). Its derivative is \(x^3\), not \(x^2\). Before a product or a quotient is integrated, the integrand is rearranged into a sum of terms, or the variable is substituted (card 9).

Integration formulas and methods

Integrals are found by the basic integration formulas, by the useful integration formulas or by substitution. The first three sections of Calculating integrals deal with these methods.

6

Basic integration formulas

Each integration formula is the corresponding derivative formula read backwards. For example, \(\left(\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\right)' = x^n\), so \(\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\) is an antiderivative of \(x^n\).

1Constants and powers
  • \(\displaystyle\int 0\, dx = C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int k\, dx = kx + C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int x^n\, dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{x}\, dx = \ln|x| + C\)

In the power formula \(n \ne -1\).

2Exponential functions
  • \(\displaystyle\int e^x\, dx = e^x + C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int a^x\, dx = \frac{a^x}{\ln a} + C\)

Here \(a > 0\), \(a \ne 1\).

3Trigonometric functions
  • \(\displaystyle\int \sin x\, dx = -\cos x + C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \cos x\, dx = \sin x + C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\cos^2 x}\, dx = \tan x + C\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\sin^2 x}\, dx = -\cot x + C\)

When \(n = -1\), the power formula cannot be used, because it would mean dividing by zero. Therefore the function \(\dfrac{1}{x} = x^{-1}\) is integrated by a separate formula. The absolute value in \(\ln|x|\) is needed because \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) is defined for negative \(x\) too, while the logarithm is defined only for positive numbers.

Roots and fractions as powers

Roots and fractions with a power of \(x\) in the denominator are first written as powers. Then the power formula is applied:

\[ \sqrt{x} = x^{\frac{1}{2}}; \qquad \sqrt[n]{x^m} = x^{\frac{m}{n}}; \qquad \frac{1}{x^n} = x^{-n} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \sqrt{x} = x^{\frac{1}{2}}; \qquad \sqrt[n]{x^m} = x^{\frac{m}{n}}; \\ \frac{1}{x^n} = x^{-n} \end{gathered} \]

Example

Find the integral \(\displaystyle\int\left(2e^x + \frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}}\right) dx\).

Each term is integrated separately. The second term is written as a power, \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}} = x^{-\frac{1}{4}}\). The new exponent is \(-\dfrac{1}{4} + 1 = \dfrac{3}{4}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\int\left(2e^x + x^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right) dx \\ &= 2\int e^x\, dx + \int x^{-\frac{1}{4}}\, dx \\ &= 2e^x + \frac{x^{\frac{3}{4}}}{\frac{3}{4}} + C \\ &= 2e^x + \frac{4}{3}\sqrt[4]{x^3} + C \end{aligned} \]

Check. \(\left(2e^x + \frac{4}{3}x^{\frac{3}{4}} + C\right)'\) \(= 2e^x + \frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{3}{4}x^{-\frac{1}{4}}\) \(= 2e^x + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}}\).

7

Useful integration formulas

Instead of \(x\), the argument of the integrand can be a linear function \(ax + b\), for example \(\cos(5x + 8)\) or \(e^{-0.2x}\). Such integrals are found by one rule.

If \(F(u)\) is an antiderivative of \(f(u)\) and \(a \ne 0\), then

\[ \int f(ax + b)\, dx = \frac{1}{a}F(ax + b) + C \]

The basic formula is applied as if \(ax + b\) were the variable of integration, and the result is divided by \(a\), the coefficient of \(x\). The constant term \(b\) does not change the divisor.

Why the result is divided by \(a\)

Differentiate the right-hand side by the chain rule. The derivative of the inner function, \((ax + b)' = a\), cancels with the factor \(\frac{1}{a}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\left(\frac{1}{a}F(ax + b)\right)' \\ &= \frac{1}{a}\cdot F'(ax + b)\cdot a \\ &= F'(ax + b) = f(ax + b) \end{aligned} \]

Applying this rule to the basic formulas gives the useful integration formulas (\(a \ne 0\)):

1A power, a root and a fraction
  • \(\displaystyle\int (ax + b)^n\, dx = \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{(ax + b)^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int (ax + b)^n\, dx \\ &= \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{(ax + b)^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\end{aligned}\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{ax + b}}\, dx = \frac{1}{a}\cdot 2\sqrt{ax + b} + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{ax + b}}\, dx \\ &= \frac{1}{a}\cdot 2\sqrt{ax + b} + C\end{aligned}\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{ax + b}\, dx = \frac{1}{a}\ln|ax + b| + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int \frac{1}{ax + b}\, dx \\ &= \frac{1}{a}\ln|ax + b| + C\end{aligned}\)

In the power formula \(n \ne -1\).

2Exponential functions
  • \(\displaystyle\int e^{ax + b}\, dx = \frac{1}{a}e^{ax + b} + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int e^{ax + b}\, dx \\ &= \frac{1}{a}e^{ax + b} + C\end{aligned}\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int k^{mx + n}\, dx = \frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{k^{mx + n}}{\ln k} + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int k^{mx + n}\, dx \\ &= \frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{k^{mx + n}}{\ln k} + C\end{aligned}\)

The base of the exponential function is denoted \(k\) (\(k > 0\), \(k \ne 1\)), and \(m\) is the non-zero coefficient of \(x\).

3Trigonometric functions
  • \(\displaystyle\int \sin(ax + b)\, dx = -\frac{1}{a}\cos(ax + b) + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int \sin(ax + b)\, dx \\ &= -\frac{1}{a}\cos(ax + b) + C\end{aligned}\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \cos(ax + b)\, dx = \frac{1}{a}\sin(ax + b) + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int \cos(ax + b)\, dx \\ &= \frac{1}{a}\sin(ax + b) + C\end{aligned}\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\cos^2(ax + b)}\, dx = \frac{1}{a}\tan(ax + b) + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int \frac{1}{\cos^2(ax + b)}\, dx \\ &= \frac{1}{a}\tan(ax + b) + C\end{aligned}\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\sin^2(ax + b)}\, dx = -\frac{1}{a}\cot(ax + b) + C\)\(\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int \frac{1}{\sin^2(ax + b)}\, dx \\ &= -\frac{1}{a}\cot(ax + b) + C\end{aligned}\)

When \(a = 1\) (or \(m = 1\)) and the constant term is zero, the basic formulas are obtained.

Example

Find the integral \(\displaystyle\int \cos(5x + 8)\, dx\).

The inner function is \(5x + 8\), so \(a = 5\). The antiderivative of the cosine is the sine: the argument \(5x + 8\) is kept unchanged, and the result is divided by 5:

\[ \int \cos(5x + 8)\, dx = \frac{1}{5}\sin(5x + 8) + C \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\int \cos(5x + 8)\, dx \\ &= \frac{1}{5}\sin(5x + 8) + C \end{aligned} \]

Check. \(\left(\frac{1}{5}\sin(5x + 8)\right)'\) \(= \frac{1}{5}\cos(5x + 8)\cdot 5\) \(= \cos(5x + 8)\).

Important. The useful formulas are used only when the inner function is linear. In \(\left(x^2 + 1\right)^3\) and \(e^{x^2}\) the derivative of the inner function is not a constant, so such integrals are found in another way, for example by substitution (card 9) or by expanding.

8

The differential

Integration by substitution uses the concept of the differential.

If \(y = f(x)\) is a differentiable function, its differential \(dy\) is the product of the derivative \(f'(x)\) and the change of the argument \(dx = \Delta x\):

\[ dy = f'(x)\, dx \]

What the differential shows. Draw the tangent line to the graph of the function at the point \(P\). When the argument changes by \(dx\), the value of the function changes by \(\Delta y\) (along the graph we rise to the point \(Q\)), and the tangent line changes by \(dy\) (to the point \(R\)). The slope of the tangent line is \(f'(x)\), so its change is \(f'(x)\, dx\). So the differential is the change along the tangent line.

When \(dx\) is small, the graph and the tangent line almost coincide, so \(\Delta y \approx dy\). This is the same approximation \(\Delta y \approx f'(x)\cdot\Delta x\) that was used in Topic 6. For example, for the consumption function \(C(x) = 100 + 0.9x - 0.0001x^2\), when the income \(x = 1000\) euros increases by \(dx = 20\) euros, the differential is \(dC = C'(1000)\cdot dx\) \(= 0.7\cdot 20 = 14\) euros, while the exact change is \(\Delta C = C(1020) - C(1000)\) \(= 913.96 - 900 = 13.96\) euros.

The differential in the substitution method

In the substitution method the new variable is denoted \(a\). If \(a\) is a function of \(x\), its differential is written as follows:

\[ da = a'\, dx \]

For example, if \(a = x^2 + 5\), then \(da = \left(x^2 + 5\right)'dx = 2x\, dx\). From this equality \(dx\) is expressed by dividing both sides by the derivative \(a' = 2x\) (when it is not zero): \(dx = \dfrac{da}{2x}\).

The symbol \(dx\) in \(\int f(x)\, dx\) shows the variable of integration. After the substitution the integration is done with respect to the new variable, so the integral ends with \(da\).

9

Integration by substitution

Some integrals, for example \(\int 4x\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{100} dx\), cannot be found directly by the formulas, and expanding \(\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{100}\) would be impractical. Then the method of integration by substitution is used: a part of the integrand is replaced by a new variable, and the integral is rewritten so that the known formulas can be applied.

When substitution is applied

  1. The argument of a function differs from the variable of integration. The new variable is this argument. For example, in the integral \(\int 6xe^{x^2 + 2}\, dx\) the argument of the exponential function is \(x^2 + 2\), while the variable of integration is \(x\), so \(a = x^2 + 2\).
  2. The integrand includes a function and its (almost) derivative. The new variable is the function whose (almost) derivative is in the integrand. For example, the integral \(\int 4x\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{100} dx\) includes the function \(x^2 - 3\) and the factor \(4x\), and \(\left(x^2 - 3\right)' = 2x\), so \(a = x^2 - 3\).

An almost derivative is the derivative multiplied by a constant factor. If the derivative is \(2x\), both \(4x = 2\cdot 2x\) and \(x = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 2x\) are suitable. In both cases we obtain the general formula:

\[ \int f\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x)\, dx = \int f(u)\, du = F(u) + C \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\int f\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x)\, dx \\ &= \int f(u)\, du = F(u) + C \end{aligned} \]

When the argument is linear, for example \(\cos(5x + 8)\), substitution also works, but the useful formulas are more convenient (card 7).

Writing the solution

  1. Next to the integral, in square brackets, introduce the new variable \(a = \ldots\), calculate the differential \(da = a'\, dx\) and express \(dx\) from it.
  2. In the integrand, replace the chosen expression with \(a\), and \(dx\) with the expression obtained.
  3. Simplify the expression so that only the variable \(a\) remains.
  4. Integrate with respect to \(a\) by the known formulas.
  5. Return to the original variable: replace \(a\) with its expression in \(x\).

Example

Find the integral \(\displaystyle\int 4x\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{100} dx\).

The derivative of the function \(x^2 - 3\), equal to \(2x\), is in the integrand as the factor \(4x = 2\cdot 2x\) (an almost derivative). The new variable is \(a = x^2 - 3\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \int 4x\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{100} dx &= \left[\begin{array}{l} a = x^2 - 3 \\ da = a'\,dx = 2x\, dx \;\;|:2x \\ dx = \dfrac{da}{2x} \end{array}\right] \\ &= \int 4x\cdot a^{100}\cdot\frac{da}{2x} = 2\int a^{100}\, da \\ &= 2\cdot\frac{a^{101}}{101} + C = \frac{2}{101}\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{101} + C \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\int 4x\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{100} dx \\ &= \left[\begin{array}{l} a = x^2 - 3 \\ da = a'dx = 2x\,dx \;|:2x \\ dx = \dfrac{da}{2x} \end{array}\right] \\ &= \int 4x\cdot a^{100}\cdot\frac{da}{2x} \\ &= 2\int a^{100}\, da \\ &= 2\cdot\frac{a^{101}}{101} + C \\ &= \frac{2}{101}\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{101} + C \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\dfrac{4x}{2x} = 2\), the variable \(x\) cancels and only \(a\) remains in the integral. In the last step \(a\) was replaced with \(x^2 - 3\).

Check. \(\left(\frac{2}{101}\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{101}\right)'\) \(= \frac{2}{101}\cdot 101\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{100}\cdot 2x\) \(= 4x\left(x^2 - 3\right)^{100}\).
