Key Concepts

1

What is a system of linear equations?

A system of linear equations consists of several linear equations in the same variables. In a linear equation the variables appear only to the first power: they are not raised to powers and are not multiplied by one another.

A solution of the system is a set of values of the variables that satisfies all the equations of the system at the same time.

Example \[ \begin{cases} 2x - 3y = -8 \\ 4x + y = -2 \end{cases} \]

The pair \(x = -1\), \(y = 2\) is a solution of this system, because it satisfies both equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2\cdot(-1) - 3\cdot 2 &= -8 \\ 4\cdot(-1) + 2 &= -2 \end{aligned} \]

The solution is written in brackets in the order of the variables: \((-1;\,2)\).

General form

In general, a system of three equations in three variables is written as follows:

\[ \begin{cases} a_{11}x + a_{12}y + a_{13}z = b_1 \\ a_{21}x + a_{22}y + a_{23}z = b_2 \\ a_{31}x + a_{32}y + a_{33}z = b_3 \end{cases} \]

In the coefficient \(a_{ij}\) the first index \(i\) is the number of the equation, and the second index \(j\) is the number of the variable (1 – \(x\), 2 – \(y\), 3 – \(z\)). The numbers \(b_1\), \(b_2\), \(b_3\) are the right-hand sides of the equations.

The order of the indices is the same as for the matrix entry \(a_{ij}\): first the row (the equation), then the column (the variable).

2

Augmented matrix

The coefficients of the system are written as an augmented matrix: each equation becomes one row of the matrix, and the right-hand sides of the equations are written after a vertical bar. The variables disappear, so only the numbers have to be carried through the calculation.

\(\begin{cases} \htmlClass{hc}{a_{11}}x + a_{12}y + a_{13}z = \htmlClass{hd}{b_1} \\ \htmlClass{hc}{a_{21}}x + a_{22}y + a_{23}z = \htmlClass{hd}{b_2} \\ \htmlClass{hc}{a_{31}}x + a_{32}y + a_{33}z = \htmlClass{hd}{b_3} \end{cases}\) ↓ \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hc}{a_{11}} & a_{12} & a_{13} & \htmlClass{hd}{b_1} \\ \htmlClass{hc}{a_{21}} & a_{22} & a_{23} & \htmlClass{hd}{b_2} \\ \htmlClass{hc}{a_{31}} & a_{32} & a_{33} & \htmlClass{hd}{b_3} \end{array}\right)\)

The coefficients of the variable \(x\) are highlighted in green – they make up the first column. The right-hand sides are highlighted in orange – they are written after the bar.

How to transfer the coefficients

  • Each equation is one row, and each variable is one column.
  • Write the variables in the same order in every equation (for example, \(x, y, z, w\)).
  • If there is no number in front of a variable, its coefficient is \(1\) (or \(-1\) if there is a minus sign in front of it).
  • If a variable does not appear in an equation, its coefficient is \(0\) – the zero must not be left out.
  • The variables must be on the left-hand side of the equation and the numbers on the right-hand side. If a variable is written on the right-hand side, first move it to the left: for example, \(y = 2x\) is written as \(-2x + y = 0\).

Example

The augmented matrix of a system in four variables:

\(\begin{cases} x - y - 4z + 9w = 22 \\ 2x - 3y + z + 5w = -3 \\ x + 2y - 4w = -3 \\ 3x - 2y - 5z + w = 3 \end{cases}\) ↓ \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & \htmlClass{hd}{-1} & -4 & 9 & 22 \\ 2 & -3 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 5 & -3 \\ \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 2 & \htmlClass{hd}{0} & -4 & -3 \\ 3 & -2 & -5 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 3 \end{array}\right)\)

The coefficients highlighted in orange are not written in the equations: the ones in front of the variables and the \(0\) in the third row, because the third equation does not contain \(z\). The columns correspond in turn to the variables \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), \(w\).

The augmented matrix has as many rows as there are equations in the system, and one column more than there are variables.

3

Elementary row operations

A system having the same solutions as the original system is an equivalent system (written \(\sim\)). Three operations do not change the set of solutions of a system – they are the elementary row operations:

1. Interchange any two rows

\(\begin{cases} 2x - 3y = -8 \\ 4x + y = -2 \end{cases}\) \(\sim\) \(\begin{cases} 4x + y = -2 \\ 2x - 3y = -8 \end{cases}\)

2. Multiply a row by a nonzero constant

\(\begin{cases} 2x - 3y = -8 & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert\cdot(-2)} \\ 4x + y = -2 \end{cases}\) \(\sim\) \(\begin{cases} -4x + 6y = 16 \\ 4x + y = -2 \end{cases}\)

3. Add a multiple of one row to another row

\(\begin{cases} 2x - 3y = -8 & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert\cdot(-2)} \\ 4x + y = -2 & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{cases}\) \(\sim\) \(\begin{cases} 2x - 3y = -8 \\ 0x + 7y = 14 \end{cases}\)

The first equation multiplied by \(-2\) is added to the second: \((-4x + 6y) + (4x + y) = 16 + (-2)\), that is, \(7y = 14\). The first equation stays unchanged, and \(x\) has disappeared from the second.

The same operations in the augmented matrix

In the augmented matrix each equation is a row, so the operations are carried out on rows. The operation being carried out is written beside the matrix: a number in brackets is the factor by which the row it stands next to is multiplied, and the arrow shows the row to which the result is then added; \({:}\,k\) beside a row means that the row is divided by \(k\).

\(\left(\begin{array}{cc|c} 2 & -3 & -8 \\ 4 & 1 & -2 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \htmlClass{ha}{(-2)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cc|c} 2 & -3 & -8 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & 7 & 14 \end{array}\right)\)

The new second row is obtained column by column – including the number after the bar:

Row 241−2
Row 1 · (−2)−4616
New row 20714

Only the row the arrow points to changes. The row that was multiplied stays the same.

One variable is left in the second row: \(7y = 14 \;\vert\, {:}\,7\), so \(y = 2\). Substituting \(y = 2\) into the first equation gives \(2x - 3\cdot 2 = -8\), \(x = -1\) – the solution \((-1;\,2)\) we have already seen. Eliminating variables by row operations until an equation with one variable is left – this is the idea of Gaussian elimination.

?Why do the row operations not change the solutions?

If numbers satisfy both equations, they also satisfy the equation obtained by multiplying one equation by a number or by adding it to another. Moreover, every operation can be undone: divide by the same number, interchange the equations back, subtract the same equation. So no solutions appear and none are lost during the operations.

Multiplying by \(0\) is not allowed, because the equation would turn into \(0 = 0\) and its information would be lost – such an operation can no longer be undone.

Gaussian elimination

4

The idea of the method: triangular form

The goal of Gaussian elimination is to transform the system into triangular form: using elementary row operations, all the entries of the augmented matrix below the main diagonal are turned into zero. The 2nd and 3rd operations are used for this, and if necessary also the 1st (interchanging rows).

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{a_{11}} & a_{12} & a_{13} & b_1 \\ a_{21} & \htmlClass{hd}{a_{22}} & a_{23} & b_2 \\ a_{31} & a_{32} & \htmlClass{hd}{a_{33}} & b_3 \end{array}\right)\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & a'_{12} & a'_{13} & b'_1 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & a''_{23} & b''_2 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & b'''_3 \end{array}\right)\)

The main diagonal is highlighted in orange, and the zeros obtained below it in green. The primes (\(a'\), \(b''\), …) show that the values of the entries have changed after the operations.

The last matrix corresponds to the triangular system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} x + a'_{12}y + a'_{13}z &= b'_1 \\ y + a''_{23}z &= b''_2 \\ z &= b'''_3 \end{aligned} \right. \]

Such a system is easy to solve: only one variable, \(z\), is left in the last equation. Substituting it into the second equation gives \(y\), and substituting \(y\) and \(z\) into the first gives \(x\).

5

The Gaussian elimination algorithm

I. Forward elimination

  1. Pivot element. Look at the first coefficient of the first equation. If it is 0, interchange this equation with an equation below it whose first coefficient is not 0. This non-zero coefficient will be the pivot element.
  2. A 1 on the diagonal. Divide the first equation by the pivot element – a 1 will appear in its place.
  3. Zeros below the pivot element. Multiply the first equation by a suitable number and add it to each equation below it (the 2nd and 3rd operations) – this eliminates the first variable from them.
  4. Repetition. Set the first equation aside and repeat steps 1–3 with the remaining equations: now the second variable is eliminated, then the third, and so on.
  5. End. Forward elimination is finished when there are only zeros below the main diagonal and ones on the diagonal. If an equation \(0 = 0\) has appeared on the way, it can be deleted. If an equation \(0 = a\) with \(a \neq 0\) has appeared, there is no need to continue – the system has no solution.

II. Back substitution

  1. Values from bottom to top. Find the last variable from the last equation and substitute it into the equation above it. Moving upwards in this way, find the values of all the variables.

The algorithm is based on: V. Būda, J. Granskas. Diskretieji matematiniai modeliai: ekonomika ir vadyba. Vilnius, TEV, 2015, Chapter 1.

When the pivot element equals 1, it is also called a leading 1.

?General scheme: 3 equations in 3 variables

This is how the algorithm looks in the augmented matrix. The first row is divided by \(a_{11}\) and then used to obtain zeros in the first column; the same is repeated with the second row, and at the end the third row is divided by its pivot element:

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{a_{11}} & a_{12} & a_{13} & b_1 \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & a_{23} & b_2 \\ a_{31} & a_{32} & a_{33} & b_3 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,a_{11}} \\ \phantom{0} \\ \phantom{0} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & a'_{12} & a'_{13} & b'_1 \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & a_{23} & b_2 \\ a_{31} & a_{32} & a_{33} & b_3 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{cc} \htmlClass{ha}{(-a_{21})} & \htmlClass{ha}{(-a_{31})} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} \\ & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & a'_{12} & a'_{13} & b'_1 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hd}{a'_{22}} & a'_{23} & b'_2 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & a'_{32} & a'_{33} & b'_3 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,a'_{22}} \\ \phantom{0} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & a'_{12} & a'_{13} & b'_1 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & a''_{23} & b''_2 \\ 0 & a'_{32} & a'_{33} & b'_3 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{(-a'_{32})} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & a'_{12} & a'_{13} & b'_1 \\ 0 & 1 & a''_{23} & b''_2 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hd}{a''_{33}} & b''_3 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,a''_{33}} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & a'_{12} & a'_{13} & b'_1 \\ 0 & 1 & a''_{23} & b''_2 \\ 0 & 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & b'''_3 \end{array}\right)\)

When the pivot element equals 1, an entry below it is turned into zero by multiplying the pivot row by the opposite number: \(a_{21}\) is eliminated by the factor \((-a_{21})\), and \(a_{31}\) by the factor \((-a_{31})\).

Practical tips

  • The factor. When the pivot element equals 1, a number \(a\) below it is eliminated by the factor \((-a)\): a 2 below the 1 by the factor \((-2)\), a 4 by the factor \((-4)\).
  • Interchange or divide? Interchanging rows is often easier than dividing: if a row below has a 1 in the required position, move it up (see Example 4).
  • When dividing would give fractions, the pivot row need not be divided. What matters is to obtain zeros below the main diagonal: the factors are chosen so that the sum is 0 (see Examples 1 and 2).
  • Decimals. If an equation contains decimals or percentages, multiply the whole row by 10, 100 or 1000 first. This is an elementary row operation too, so the solutions do not change.
  • Check. Substitute the values obtained into the original equations – this way a calculation error is easy to spot.
!Common mistakes
  • The number after the bar forgotten. An operation applies to the whole row, including the right-hand side.
  • The wrong row changed. Only the row the arrow points to changes; the row that was multiplied stays the same.
  • A sign error. When a row is multiplied by a negative number, the signs of all its entries change.
  • A missing zero. If a variable does not appear in an equation, a \(0\) is written in its place in the matrix; without it the columns shift.
  • Back substitution from the top. It starts from the last equation, in which only one variable is left.
What you must write in the exam
  1. The solution must include the mathematical model of the problem – the system of linear equations. Presenting only the augmented matrix is not sufficient.
  2. The system of equations must be solved using the Gaussian elimination method. The solution must show which row operations are performed to eliminate elements below the main diagonal.
  3. At the end of the solution, the final answer must be clearly presented.

Number of solutions

6

Three possible cases

A system of linear equations can have exactly one solution, no solution or infinitely many solutions. There are no other possibilities: for example, a system cannot have exactly two solutions.

This is easy to see when there are two equations in two variables: each equation represents a line in the plane, and a solution is a common point of the two lines.

When there are three variables, each equation represents a plane in space. The planes can intersect at one point (exactly one solution), have a common line (infinitely many solutions) or have no point common to all of them (no solution).

How to recognise the case from the matrix

Which case we have becomes clear when forward elimination is finished (\(\ast\) denotes any number):

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & \ast & \ast & \ast \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & \ast & \ast \\ 0 & 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & \ast \end{array}\right)\)Exactly one solution
\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & \ast & \ast & \ast \\ 0 & 1 & \ast & \ast \\ \htmlClass{hr}{0} & \htmlClass{hr}{0} & \htmlClass{hr}{0} & \htmlClass{hr}{c} \end{array}\right)\)No solution (\(c \neq 0\))
\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & \ast & \ast & \ast \\ 0 & 1 & \ast & \ast \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} \end{array}\right)\)Infinitely many solutions
  • Exactly one solution – every variable has its own non-zero pivot element (if the rows were not divided, it need not equal 1), and there are no impossible rows and no rows of zeros.
  • No solution – a row of the form \(0 \;\; 0 \;\; 0 \mid c\) with \(c \neq 0\) appears. It reads \(0 = c\), which is impossible.
  • Infinitely many solutions – a row of zeros \(0 \;\; 0 \;\; 0 \mid 0\) appears (the equation \(0 = 0\)), which can be deleted, and fewer non-zero rows than variables remain.

How to write infinitely many solutions

  1. Delete the rows of zeros.
  2. Count the free variables: their number is the number of variables minus the number of non-zero rows.
  3. Set the variables that have no pivot element equal to parameters: \(w = t\), \(t \in \mathbb{R}\) (if more are needed – \(u\) and so on).
  4. Move the parameters to the right-hand side and express the other variables through them by back substitution.

The answer states which values the parameter can take, for example, \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). Such a form covers all the solutions. Substituting a specific value of the parameter gives one particular solution (see Example 4).

What you must write in the exam
  1. If the system has infinitely many solutions, they must be expressed using a parameter. In applied problems, it is necessary to indicate the interval of possible parameter values. If required by the problem, particular solutions must also be provided.

Example 3

A system with no solution

Problem Solve the system by Gaussian elimination \[ \begin{cases} x + y + z = 2 \\ 2x - 3y + z = 11 \\ 4x - y + 3z = 10 \end{cases} \]

1Zeros in the first column

The first pivot element equals 1. We multiply the first row by \(-2\) and add it to the second, then multiply it by \(-4\) and add it to the third:

The last row reads

\[ 0x + 0y + 0z = -5, \quad \text{i.e.} \quad 0 = -5 \]

There are no \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) for which this equality would be true. Further calculations are unnecessary.

Answer

The system has no solution: \(\varnothing\).

Example 4

A system with infinitely many solutions

Problem Solve the system by Gaussian elimination \[ \begin{cases} 2x - y - z + 3w = 3 \\ x - y + w = 1 \\ 3x - 4y + 2z + w = 4 \\ x + y - 3z + 4w = 1 \end{cases} \]

1Interchanging the equations

The first coefficient of the first equation is 2, and that of the second is 1. Instead of dividing, we interchange the 1st and 2nd equations and write the augmented matrix:

\[ \left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & -1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & -1 & 3 & 3 \\ 3 & -4 & 2 & 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & -3 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right) \]

2Zeros in the first column

\(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & -1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & -1 & 3 & 3 \\ 3 & -4 & 2 & 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & -3 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{ccc} \htmlClass{ha}{(-2)} & \htmlClass{ha}{(-3)} & \htmlClass{ha}{(-1)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} \\ & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} \\ & & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & -1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & 1 & -1 & 1 & 1 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -1 & 2 & -2 & 1 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & 2 & -3 & 3 & 0 \end{array}\right)\)

3Zeros in the second column

The second pivot element equals 1: the \(-1\) below it is eliminated by the factor \((1)\), and the 2 by the factor \((-2)\).

\(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & -1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & -1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & -3 & 3 & 0 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{cc} \phantom{0} & \\ \htmlClass{ha}{(1)} & \htmlClass{ha}{(-2)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} \\ & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & -1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & 1 & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -1 & 1 & -2 \end{array}\right)\)

4A row of zeros

Adding the third row to the fourth turns the fourth row into a row of zeros:

\(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & -1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 & -2 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \phantom{0} \\ \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{(1)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & -1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -1 & 2 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} \end{array}\right)\)

The equation \(0 = 0\) is true for any values, so we delete it.

5The parameter

3 non-zero rows are left and there are 4 variables, so there is one free variable: \(4 - 3 = 1\). The variable \(w\) has no pivot element, so we put \(w = t\), \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), and move the terms with \(t\) to the right-hand side:

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & -1 & 0 & 1 - t \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 1 - t \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 + t \end{array}\right) \]

6Back substitution

\[ \begin{aligned} &z = 2 + t \\ &y - (2 + t) = 1 - t \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 3 \\ &x - 3 = 1 - t \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 4 - t \end{aligned} \]

Check with \(t = 0\), i.e. \((4;\, 3;\, 2;\, 0)\): \(8 - 3 - 2 + 0 = 3\), \(4 - 3 + 0 = 1\), \(12 - 12 + 4 + 0 = 4\), \(4 + 3 - 6 + 0 = 1\).

Answer \[ \begin{gathered} (x;\, y;\, z;\, w) = (4 - t;\, 3;\, 2 + t;\, t), \\ t \in \mathbb{R} \end{gathered} \]

Each value of the parameter \(t\) gives a different solution, so there are infinitely many solutions. For example, particular solutions: \(t = 0\) gives \((4;\, 3;\, 2;\, 0)\), and \(t = 1\) gives \((3;\, 3;\, 3;\, 1)\). Note that \(y = 3\) in all the solutions.

7

The number of equations and variables

In general, the number of equations in a system need not be equal to the number of variables.

Number of equationsPossible cases
Less than the number of variablesinfinitely many solutions or no solution
Greater than the number of variablesexactly one solution, infinitely many solutions or no solution

When there are fewer equations than variables, there cannot be exactly one solution: there will be fewer non-zero rows than variables, so if the system has solutions, at least one free variable will remain. When there are as many equations as variables, all three cases are possible – as Examples 1, 3 and 4 show.

The number of free variables (parameters) is determined not by the original number of equations but by the number of non-zero rows left after forward elimination. If an equation has turned into a row of zeros, there will be more parameters – see Example 5.

Example 5

Three equations in four variables

Problem Solve the system by Gaussian elimination \[ \begin{cases} x + y + z + w = 6 \\ 3x + 2y + z + w = 1 \\ 5x + 4y + 3z + 3w = 13 \end{cases} \]

1Zeros in the first column

\(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 5 & 4 & 3 & 3 & 13 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{cc} \htmlClass{ha}{(-3)} & \htmlClass{ha}{(-5)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} \\ & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -1 & -2 & -2 & -17 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -1 & -2 & -2 & -17 \end{array}\right)\)

2A row of zeros

The second and third rows are the same. Multiplying the second row by \(-1\) and adding it to the third gives a row of zeros, which we delete:

\(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{-1} & -2 & -2 & -17 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 & -2 & -17 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{(-1)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 & -2 & -17 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hc}{0} \end{array}\right)\)

3Two parameters

2 non-zero rows are left and there are 4 variables, so there are two free variables: \(4 - 2 = 2\). The variables \(z\) and \(w\) have no pivot elements, so we put \(w = t\), \(z = u\), \(t, u \in \mathbb{R}\), and move the terms with the parameters to the right-hand side:

\(\left(\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 6 - t - u \\ 0 & -1 & -17 + 2t + 2u \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,(-1)} \end{array}\)

4Back substitution

\[ \begin{aligned} &y = 17 - 2t - 2u \\ &x + 17 - 2t - 2u = 6 - t - u \\ &\Longrightarrow\; x = -11 + t + u \end{aligned} \]
Answer \[ \begin{gathered} (x;\, y;\, z;\, w) = {} \\ (-11 + t + u;\; 17 - 2t - 2u;\; u;\; t), \\ t, u \in \mathbb{R} \end{gathered} \]

Although there were three equations, one of them gave no new information (it turned into a row of zeros), so there are two parameters, not one.
