Standard Constrained Optimization Problems

This section solves applied problems in which the variables are connected by an additional condition – a constraint: a set quantity has to be produced, an order has to be filled or the whole budget has to be spent. Such problems are solved by the method of Lagrange multipliers, which is described in the Topic 9 part “Constrained optimization”.

Building the model

The solution of a constrained optimization problem starts with the model (see “The Lagrange problem”). We build it by answering three questions.

  1. Which quantities can be chosen? These are the variables \(x\) and \(y\), for example the quantities of two products, the amounts delivered from two warehouses, hours of labour and machine hours.
  2. Which quantity has to be maximized or minimized? This is the objective function \(f(x;\,y)\): profit, cost, utility or output. It may be given in the problem, or it has to be built from parts.
  3. What condition must the variables satisfy? This is the constraint \(g(x;\,y) = m\). The constant \(m\) may be a budget, a required amount or a capacity.

The most common constraints

In the problemConstraint
A total of exactly \(m\) units has to be produced or delivered\(x + y = m\)
The whole budget \(m\) is spent on two inputs with the unit prices \(c_1\) and \(c_2\)\(c_1x + c_2y = m\)
The output \(Q(x;\,y)\) has to meet the order \(m\)\(Q(x;\,y) = m\)

The objective function – given or built

A profit, cost, utility or production function is often given in the problem. In other cases we build the objective function ourselves:

  • if the cost consists of several parts, the objective function is their sum, for example the cost of transportation from two warehouses (Example 3);
  • if the unit prices of the inputs \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are known, the cost is \(TC = c_1x + c_2y\) (Examples 7 and 8).

The same production function can be either the objective function or the constraint. If a budget is given and the largest output is sought, \(Q(x;\,y)\) is the objective function and \(c_1x + c_2y = m\) is the constraint (Example 6). If an order is given and the smallest cost is sought, the objective function is \(c_1x + c_2y\) and \(Q(x;\,y) = m\) is the constraint (Example 8).

The Lagrangian function and the Lagrange multiplier

We rewrite the constraint in the form \(g(x;\,y) - m = 0\) – the constant \(m\) is moved to the left-hand side. From the objective function we subtract this expression multiplied by \(\lambda\) (see “The method of Lagrange multipliers”):

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = f(x;\,y) - \lambda\big(g(x;\,y) - m\big) \]

For example, if the budget constraint is \(8x + 2y = 200\), then \(L = U(x;\,y) - \lambda(8x + 2y - 200)\).

The value of the Lagrange multiplier at the critical point shows approximately how much the optimal value of the objective function changes when the constraint constant \(m\) increases by one unit (see “The meaning of the Lagrange multiplier”). When \(m\) changes by \(\Delta m\),

\[ \Delta f^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m \]

The change \(\Delta m\) is measured in the same units as \(m\). If the change is given in per cent, \(\Delta m\) is calculated from \(m\): for example, 10 % of 1200 is \(\Delta m = 120\).

Solving the Lagrange system of equations

The Lagrange system consists of three equations: \(L'_x = 0\), \(L'_y = 0\) and the constraint equation. There are also three unknowns: \(x\), \(y\) and \(\lambda\). The method of solution depends on whether the system is linear.

When the system is linear

If the objective function is a polynomial of the second degree and the constraint is linear, for example \(x + y = m\), all three equations are linear. Such a system is conveniently solved by substitution:

  1. In the first two equations we move the term with \(\lambda\) to the other side and obtain two expressions for \(\lambda\).
  2. Both expressions are equal to the same \(\lambda\), so we set them equal. This gives an equation in \(x\) and \(y\) only.
  3. From this equation or from the constraint equation we express one variable and substitute it into the other equation. This gives an equation in one variable.
  4. Having found \(x\) and \(y\), we substitute them into one expression for \(\lambda\) and check with the other expression – the same value must be obtained.

A linear system can also be solved by Gaussian elimination: the augmented matrix is made up of the coefficients of \(x\), \(y\) and \(\lambda\) and the constant terms. The solution by Gaussian elimination is given in Examples 1–4 in collapsible boxes.

When the system is non-linear

If the objective function or the constraint contains roots, logarithms or a Cobb-Douglas function, the system is non-linear and Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it. The examples use the following methods:

  • Setting the expressions for \(\lambda\) equal. We express \(\lambda\) from the first two equations and set the expressions equal. If roots remain, we square both sides of the equation (Example 5).
  • Dividing the equations. We move the terms with \(\lambda\) to the right-hand sides and divide one equation by the other. The multiplier \(\lambda\) cancels, and powers of the same base are simplified by subtracting the exponents (Examples 6 and 8).
  • Substituting into the constraint. If the constraint contains the same expressions as the first two equations, for example \(\sqrt{x}\) and \(\sqrt{y}\), we express them through \(\lambda\) and substitute them into the constraint equation (Example 7).

In applied problems the variables – quantities or hours – are positive. Therefore the equations may be divided by the variables, and an equation whose both sides are positive may be squared.

How to solve

  1. Write the model. Define the variables, the objective function \(f(x;\,y)\) and the constraint \(g(x;\,y) = m\). State whether the largest or the smallest value of \(f(x;\,y)\) is sought subject to \(g(x;\,y) = m\).
  2. Write down the Lagrangian function \(L = f - \lambda(g - m)\).
  3. Find \(L'_x\) and \(L'_y\), set them equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system. Solve the system of three equations (see “Solving the Lagrange system of equations”). Label the point obtained with a letter, for example \(K(x_0;\,y_0)\), and write the value of the multiplier \(\lambda\) separately – it is not included in the coordinates of the point.
  4. Decide the type of the critical point. Check the sufficient condition: substitute the coordinates of the point \(K\) and the value of \(\lambda\) into the second order partial derivatives of \(L\) to get \(A = L''_{xx}(K)\), \(B = L''_{xy}(K)\), \(C = L''_{yy}(K)\) (see “The method of Lagrange multipliers”), or use the concavity of the Cobb-Douglas function (see “Cobb-Douglas functions in constrained optimization problems”).
  5. Calculate the optimal value. If you are asked to estimate its change, use \(\Delta f^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m\).
!Common mistakes
  • The constraint is set up incorrectly. For example, the prices of the inputs are left out of the budget constraint: \(x + y = 200\) instead of \(8x + 2y = 200\).
  • Wrong signs in the Lagrangian function. The constant \(m\) is moved to the left-hand side without changing its sign, or the minus in front of \(\lambda\) is forgotten.
  • The coefficient of \(\lambda\) is forgotten. The derivative of the term \(-\lambda(8x + 2y - 200)\) with respect to \(x\) is \(-8\lambda\), not \(-\lambda\).
  • The constraint equation is not included in the system. Three unknowns cannot be found from the two equations \(L'_x = 0\) and \(L'_y = 0\).
  • Gaussian elimination is applied to a non-linear system. Gaussian elimination is suitable only for systems of linear equations.
  • \(\lambda\) is not substituted into the second order derivatives. If \(\lambda\) remains in the derivatives, not only the coordinates of the critical point but also the value of \(\lambda\) are substituted.
  • \(\Delta m\) is calculated incorrectly. It is the change in the constraint constant in the same units as \(m\): for example, 10 % of 400 is 40.

Example 1

City and mountain bicycles

Problem A factory produces \(x\) city bicycles and \(y\) mountain bicycles a month. Its monthly profit, in euros, is
\[ P(x;\,y) = -0.2x^2 - 0.1y^2 - 0.1xy + 100x + 80y - 5000 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} P(x;\,y) &= -0.2x^2 - 0.1y^2 - 0.1xy \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 100x + 80y - 5000 \end{aligned} \]
The factory has to produce exactly 400 bicycles a month.
a) How many bicycles of each type should be produced to maximize the profit? What is the maximum profit?
b) How would the maximum profit change if the production capacity increased to 410 bicycles a month?

1The model

The variables: \(x\) is the number of city bicycles produced a month, and \(y\) is the number of mountain bicycles.

The objective function. The maximum profit has to be found. The profit function \(P(x;\,y)\) is given in the problem.

The constraint. Exactly 400 bicycles of both types together must be produced a month, so their numbers add up to 400: \(x + y = 400\).

We look for the maximum profit with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; P(x;\,y) = -0.2x^2 - 0.1y^2 - 0.1xy + 100x + 80y - 5000 \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 400 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; P(x;\,y) \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 400 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We rewrite the constraint so that its right-hand side is zero: we move the number 400 to the left-hand side and get \(x + y - 400 = 0\). From the objective function we subtract this expression multiplied by the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) (see the method of Lagrange multipliers):

\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= -0.2x^2 - 0.1y^2 - 0.1xy + 100x + 80y - 5000 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(x + y - 400) \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) \\ &= -0.2x^2 - 0.1y^2 - 0.1xy \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 100x + 80y - 5000 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(x + y - 400) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

We find the partial derivatives of the Lagrangian function with respect to \(x\) and \(y\). When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the terms \(-0.1y^2\), \(80y\) and \(-5000\) are constants, and \((-0.1xy)'_x = -0.1y\). The derivative of the last term \(-\lambda(x + y - 400)\) both with respect to \(x\) and with respect to \(y\) is \(-\lambda\). The derivative with respect to \(y\) is found in the same way:

\[ L'_x = -0.4x - 0.1y + 100 - \lambda;\qquad L'_y = -0.2y - 0.1x + 80 - \lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = -0.4x - 0.1y + 100 - \lambda \\ L'_y = -0.2y - 0.1x + 80 - \lambda \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} -0.4x - 0.1y + 100 - \lambda &= 0 \\ -0.2y - 0.1x + 80 - \lambda &= 0 \\ x + y &= 400 \end{aligned} \right. \]

The system has three equations and three unknowns: \(x\), \(y\) and \(\lambda\). All the unknowns are of the first degree and are not multiplied by each other, so the system is linear.

4Solving the system by substitution

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first two equations we move the term \(-\lambda\) to the right-hand side (its sign changes):

\[ \lambda = -0.4x - 0.1y + 100;\qquad \lambda = -0.2y - 0.1x + 80 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = -0.4x - 0.1y + 100 \\ \lambda = -0.2y - 0.1x + 80 \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). Both expressions are equal to the same \(\lambda\), so we set them equal:

\[ -0.4x - 0.1y + 100 = -0.2y - 0.1x + 80 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} -0.4x - 0.1y + 100 \\ = -0.2y - 0.1x + 80 \end{gathered} \]

We move the terms with the variables to the left-hand side and the numbers to the right-hand side (the signs of the terms moved change). We collect like terms and multiply both sides by 10 to remove the decimals:

\[ \begin{gathered} -0.4x + 0.1x - 0.1y + 0.2y = 80 - 100 \\ \Longrightarrow\; -0.3x + 0.1y = -20 \;\Longrightarrow\; -3x + y = -200 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} -0.4x + 0.1x - 0.1y + 0.2y = 80 - 100 \\ \Longrightarrow\; -0.3x + 0.1y = -20 \\ \Longrightarrow\; -3x + y = -200 \end{gathered} \]

From the equation obtained we express \(y\) – we move the term \(-3x\) to the right-hand side:

\[ y = 3x - 200 \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression into the constraint equation \(x + y = 400\) in place of \(y\) and solve the equation in one variable:

\[ x + (3x - 200) = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; 4x - 200 = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; 4x = 600 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 150 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x + (3x - 200) = 400 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 4x - 200 = 400 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 4x = 600 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 150 \end{gathered} \]

We substitute \(x\) into the expression \(y = 3x - 200\): \(y = 3\cdot 150 - 200 = 250\).

The value of the multiplier. We substitute the values \(x = 150\) and \(y = 250\) into the first expression for \(\lambda\) and check with the second:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda &= -0.4\cdot 150 - 0.1\cdot 250 + 100 = -60 - 25 + 100 = 15 \\ \lambda &= -0.2\cdot 250 - 0.1\cdot 150 + 80 = -50 - 15 + 80 = 15 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda &= -0.4\cdot 150 - 0.1\cdot 250 + 100 \\ &= -60 - 25 + 100 = 15 \\[4pt] \lambda &= -0.2\cdot 250 - 0.1\cdot 150 + 80 \\ &= -50 - 15 + 80 = 15 \end{aligned} \]

We obtain the critical point \(K(150;\,250)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 15\).

This system is linear, so it can also be solved by Gaussian elimination. If you prefer this method, the solution is given below.

Solution by Gaussian elimination

First we rearrange the equations: the unknowns are written on the left-hand side in the order \(x\), \(y\), \(\lambda\), and the numbers are moved to the right-hand side (their signs change). To remove the decimals, the first two equations are multiplied by \(-10\):

\[ \begin{aligned} -0.4x - 0.1y - \lambda = -100\;\big|\cdot(-10) \;&\Longrightarrow\; 4x + y + 10\lambda = 1000 \\ -0.1x - 0.2y - \lambda = -80\;\big|\cdot(-10) \;&\Longrightarrow\; x + 2y + 10\lambda = 800 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} -0.4x - 0.1y - \lambda = -100\;\big|\cdot(-10) \\ \Longrightarrow\; 4x + y + 10\lambda = 1000 \\[4pt] -0.1x - 0.2y - \lambda = -80\;\big|\cdot(-10) \\ \Longrightarrow\; x + 2y + 10\lambda = 800 \end{gathered} \]

The columns of the augmented matrix are the coefficients of the unknowns \(x\), \(y\) and \(\lambda\), and the last column contains the constant terms (see Topic 3). In the constraint equation the coefficient of \(x\) is 1, so it is written as the first row:

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 0 & 400 \\ 4 & 1 & 10 & 1000 \\ 1 & 2 & 10 & 800 \end{array}\right) \]

To get zeros below the pivot element, we multiply the first row by \(-4\) and add it to the second row, and multiply it by \(-1\) and add it to the third row:

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 0 & 400 \\ 4 & 1 & 10 & 1000 \\ 1 & 2 & 10 & 800 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{cc} \htmlClass{ha}{(-4)} & \htmlClass{ha}{(-1)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} \\ & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 400 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -3 & 10 & -600 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & 1 & 10 & 400 \end{array}\right)\)

The second element of the third row is 1. We interchange the second and the third rows – this 1 becomes the pivot element. Then we multiply the second row by 3 and add it to the third row, and divide the new third row by 40:

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 400 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 10 & 400 \\ 0 & -3 & 10 & -600 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{(3)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 400 \\ 0 & 1 & 10 & 400 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & 40 & 600 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,40} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 400 \\ 0 & 1 & 10 & 400 \\ 0 & 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 15 \end{array}\right)\)

Back substitution. The third row gives \(\lambda = 15\). The second row stands for the equation \(y + 10\lambda = 400\), and the first for \(x + y = 400\):

\[ y + 10\cdot 15 = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 250;\qquad x + 250 = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 150 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} y + 10\cdot 15 = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 250 \\ x + 250 = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 150 \end{gathered} \]

The same solution is obtained: \(x = 150\), \(y = 250\), \(\lambda = 15\).

5The sufficient condition

The second order partial derivatives of the Lagrangian function are constant and do not depend on \(\lambda\), so their values at the critical point \(K\) are the same numbers:

\[ \begin{gathered} A = L''_{xx}(K) = -0.4;\quad B = L''_{xy}(K) = -0.1;\quad C = L''_{yy}(K) = -0.2 \\ D = AC - B^2 = (-0.4)\cdot(-0.2) - (-0.1)^2 = 0.08 - 0.01 = 0.07 > 0 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -0.4 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = -0.1 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -0.2 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-0.4)\cdot(-0.2) - (-0.1)^2 \\ &= 0.08 - 0.01 = 0.07 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K(150;\,250)\) is a maximum point.

6The maximum profit and its change

a) We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the profit function:

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\max}(150;\,250) &= -0.2\cdot 150^2 - 0.1\cdot 250^2 - 0.1\cdot 150\cdot 250 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 100\cdot 150 + 80\cdot 250 - 5000 \\ &= -4500 - 6250 - 3750 + 15\,000 + 20\,000 - 5000 = 15\,500 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &P_{\max}(150;\,250) \\ &= -0.2\cdot 150^2 - 0.1\cdot 250^2 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- 0.1\cdot 150\cdot 250 + 100\cdot 150 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 80\cdot 250 - 5000 \\ &= -4500 - 6250 - 3750 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 15\,000 + 20\,000 - 5000 \\ &= 15\,500 \end{aligned} \]

b) The production capacity increases from 400 to 410 bicycles, i.e. the constraint constant increases by \(\Delta m = 10\). The Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 15\) shows that when the constraint constant increases by one bicycle, the maximum profit increases by approximately 15 euros (see the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier):

\[ \Delta P^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m = 15\cdot 10 = 150 \]
Answer

a) The factory should produce 150 city and 250 mountain bicycles a month. The maximum profit is 15 500 euros a month. b) The maximum profit would increase by approximately 150 euros.

Example 2

Day and night shifts

Problem A company has to fill an order of 500 units. It can produce \(x\) units in the day shift and \(y\) units in the night shift. The shifts share the same machines, so the cost, in euros, is
\[ TC(x;\,y) = 0.3x^2 + 0.2y^2 + 0.1xy + 20x + 30y + 2000 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} TC(x;\,y) &= 0.3x^2 + 0.2y^2 + 0.1xy \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 20x + 30y + 2000 \end{aligned} \]
a) How many units should be produced in each shift to minimize the cost? What is the minimum cost?
b) What is the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier in this problem?

1The model

The variables: \(x\) is the number of units produced in the day shift, and \(y\) is the number of units produced in the night shift.

The objective function. The minimum cost has to be found. The cost function \(TC(x;\,y)\) is given in the problem.

The constraint. The quantity produced in both shifts together must meet the order: \(x + y = 500\).

We look for the minimum cost with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; TC(x;\,y) = 0.3x^2 + 0.2y^2 + 0.1xy + 20x + 30y + 2000 \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 500 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; TC(x;\,y) \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 500 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We write the constraint in the form \(x + y - 500 = 0\). From the objective function we subtract this expression multiplied by \(\lambda\):

\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= 0.3x^2 + 0.2y^2 + 0.1xy + 20x + 30y + 2000 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(x + y - 500) \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) \\ &= 0.3x^2 + 0.2y^2 + 0.1xy \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 20x + 30y + 2000 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(x + y - 500) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the terms \(0.2y^2\), \(30y\) and \(2000\) are constants, and \((0.1xy)'_x = 0.1y\). The derivative with respect to \(y\) is found in the same way. The derivative of the term \(-\lambda(x + y - 500)\) with respect to each variable is \(-\lambda\):

\[ L'_x = 0.6x + 0.1y + 20 - \lambda;\qquad L'_y = 0.4y + 0.1x + 30 - \lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 0.6x + 0.1y + 20 - \lambda \\ L'_y = 0.4y + 0.1x + 30 - \lambda \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 0.6x + 0.1y + 20 - \lambda &= 0 \\ 0.4y + 0.1x + 30 - \lambda &= 0 \\ x + y &= 500 \end{aligned} \right. \]

All three unknowns \(x\), \(y\) and \(\lambda\) are of the first degree, so the system is linear.

4Solving the system by substitution

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first two equations we move the term \(-\lambda\) to the right-hand side:

\[ \lambda = 0.6x + 0.1y + 20;\qquad \lambda = 0.4y + 0.1x + 30 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = 0.6x + 0.1y + 20 \\ \lambda = 0.4y + 0.1x + 30 \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the two expressions equal. We move the terms with the variables to the left-hand side and the numbers to the right-hand side, collect like terms and multiply both sides by 10:

\[ 0.6x + 0.1y + 20 = 0.4y + 0.1x + 30 \;\Longrightarrow\; 0.5x - 0.3y = 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; 5x - 3y = 100 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 0.6x + 0.1y + 20 = 0.4y + 0.1x + 30 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 0.5x - 0.3y = 10 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 5x - 3y = 100 \end{gathered} \]

Substitution. This time it is more convenient to express \(y = 500 - x\) from the constraint equation \(x + y = 500\) and substitute it into the equation obtained. Since \(y\) is multiplied by \(-3\), the substituted expression is written in brackets:

\[ \begin{gathered} 5x - 3(500 - x) = 100 \;\Longrightarrow\; 5x - 1500 + 3x = 100 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 8x = 1600 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 200 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 5x - 3(500 - x) = 100 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 5x - 1500 + 3x = 100 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 8x = 1600 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 200 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(y = 500 - 200 = 300\).

The value of the multiplier. We substitute the values into the first expression for \(\lambda\) and check with the second:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda &= 0.6\cdot 200 + 0.1\cdot 300 + 20 = 120 + 30 + 20 = 170 \\ \lambda &= 0.4\cdot 300 + 0.1\cdot 200 + 30 = 120 + 20 + 30 = 170 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \lambda &= 0.6\cdot 200 + 0.1\cdot 300 + 20 \\ &= 120 + 30 + 20 = 170 \\[4pt] \lambda &= 0.4\cdot 300 + 0.1\cdot 200 + 30 \\ &= 120 + 20 + 30 = 170 \end{aligned} \]

We obtain the critical point \(K(200;\,300)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 170\).

This system is linear, so it can also be solved by Gaussian elimination. If you prefer this method, the solution is given below.

Solution by Gaussian elimination

First we rearrange the equations: the unknowns are written on the left-hand side in the order \(x\), \(y\), \(\lambda\), and the numbers are moved to the right-hand side. To remove the decimals, the first two equations are multiplied by 10:

\[ \begin{aligned} 0.6x + 0.1y - \lambda = -20\;\big|\cdot 10 \;&\Longrightarrow\; 6x + y - 10\lambda = -200 \\ 0.1x + 0.4y - \lambda = -30\;\big|\cdot 10 \;&\Longrightarrow\; x + 4y - 10\lambda = -300 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 0.6x + 0.1y - \lambda = -20\;\big|\cdot 10 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 6x + y - 10\lambda = -200 \\[4pt] 0.1x + 0.4y - \lambda = -30\;\big|\cdot 10 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x + 4y - 10\lambda = -300 \end{gathered} \]

The columns of the augmented matrix are the coefficients of the unknowns \(x\), \(y\) and \(\lambda\), and the last column contains the constant terms (see Topic 3). In the constraint equation the coefficient of \(x\) is 1, so it is written as the first row:

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 0 & 500 \\ 6 & 1 & -10 & -200 \\ 1 & 4 & -10 & -300 \end{array}\right) \]

We multiply the first row by \(-6\) and add it to the second row, and multiply it by \(-1\) and add it to the third row. We divide the new second row by \(-5\):

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 0 & 500 \\ 6 & 1 & -10 & -200 \\ 1 & 4 & -10 & -300 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{cc} \htmlClass{ha}{(-6)} & \htmlClass{ha}{(-1)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} \\ & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 500 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -5 & -10 & -3200 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & 3 & -10 & -800 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,(-5)} \\ \phantom{0} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 500 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 2 & 640 \\ 0 & 3 & -10 & -800 \end{array}\right)\)

We multiply the second row by \(-3\) and add it to the third row, and divide the new third row by \(-16\):

Example 3

Deliveries from two warehouses

Problem A retail chain must deliver 1200 pallets of goods a week to its shops from two warehouses, A and B. The weekly cost of transporting \(x\) pallets from warehouse A is \(0.01x^2 + 6x\) euros, and the cost of transporting \(y\) pallets from warehouse B is \(0.02y^2 + 3y\) euros.
a) How many pallets should be delivered from each warehouse to minimize the transportation cost? What is the minimum cost?
b) How would the minimum cost change if the weekly amount delivered increased by 10 %?

1The objective function

The variables: \(x\) is the number of pallets delivered from warehouse A a week, and \(y\) is the number of pallets delivered from warehouse B.

This time the objective function is not given in the problem – we build it ourselves. We need to find the minimum total transportation cost. It consists of two parts: the cost of transportation from warehouse A, \(0.01x^2 + 6x\), and the cost of transportation from warehouse B, \(0.02y^2 + 3y\). The total cost is the sum of these parts:

\[ TC(x;\,y) = \big(0.01x^2 + 6x\big) + \big(0.02y^2 + 3y\big) = 0.01x^2 + 6x + 0.02y^2 + 3y \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &TC(x;\,y) \\ &= \big(0.01x^2 + 6x\big) + \big(0.02y^2 + 3y\big) \\ &= 0.01x^2 + 6x + 0.02y^2 + 3y \end{aligned} \]

2The model

The constraint. The number of pallets delivered from both warehouses together must equal the required amount: \(x + y = 1200\).

We look for the minimum total cost with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; TC(x;\,y) = 0.01x^2 + 6x + 0.02y^2 + 3y \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 1200 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; TC(x;\,y) \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 1200 \end{aligned} \]

3The Lagrangian function

We write the constraint in the form \(x + y - 1200 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = 0.01x^2 + 6x + 0.02y^2 + 3y - \lambda(x + y - 1200) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= 0.01x^2 + 6x + 0.02y^2 + 3y \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(x + y - 1200) \end{aligned} \]

4The system of equations

The cost of transportation from warehouse A depends only on \(x\), and from warehouse B only on \(y\). Therefore \(L'_x\) does not depend on \(y\), and \(L'_y\) does not depend on \(x\):

\[ L'_x = 0.02x + 6 - \lambda;\qquad L'_y = 0.04y + 3 - \lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 0.02x + 6 - \lambda \\ L'_y = 0.04y + 3 - \lambda \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 0.02x + 6 - \lambda &= 0 \\ 0.04y + 3 - \lambda &= 0 \\ x + y &= 1200 \end{aligned} \right. \]

All the unknowns are of the first degree, so the system is linear.

5Solving the system by substitution

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first two equations we move the term \(-\lambda\) to the right-hand side:

\[ \lambda = 0.02x + 6;\qquad \lambda = 0.04y + 3 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = 0.02x + 6 \\ \lambda = 0.04y + 3 \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the two expressions equal. From the equation obtained we express \(x\): we move the number 6 to the right-hand side and then divide both sides by \(0.02\):

\[ 0.02x + 6 = 0.04y + 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; 0.02x = 0.04y - 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 2y - 150 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 0.02x + 6 = 0.04y + 3 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 0.02x = 0.04y - 3 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 2y - 150 \end{gathered} \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression \(x = 2y - 150\) into the constraint equation \(x + y = 1200\):

\[ (2y - 150) + y = 1200 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3y - 150 = 1200 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3y = 1350 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 450 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} (2y - 150) + y = 1200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 3y - 150 = 1200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 3y = 1350 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 450 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(x = 2\cdot 450 - 150 = 750\).

The value of the multiplier. We substitute the values into both expressions for \(\lambda\) – the same value is obtained:

\[ \lambda = 0.02\cdot 750 + 6 = 21;\qquad \lambda = 0.04\cdot 450 + 3 = 21 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = 0.02\cdot 750 + 6 = 21 \\ \lambda = 0.04\cdot 450 + 3 = 21 \end{gathered} \]

We obtain the critical point \(K(750;\,450)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 21\).

This system is linear, so it can also be solved by Gaussian elimination. If you prefer this method, the solution is given below.

First we rearrange the equations: the unknowns are written on the left-hand side, and the numbers are moved to the right-hand side. The coefficients are hundredths, so the first two equations are multiplied by 100:

\[ \begin{aligned} 0.02x - \lambda = -6\;\big|\cdot 100 \;&\Longrightarrow\; 2x - 100\lambda = -600 \\ 0.04y - \lambda = -3\;\big|\cdot 100 \;&\Longrightarrow\; 4y - 100\lambda = -300 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 0.02x - \lambda = -6\;\big|\cdot 100 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 2x - 100\lambda = -600 \\[4pt] 0.04y - \lambda = -3\;\big|\cdot 100 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 4y - 100\lambda = -300 \end{gathered} \]

The second equation does not contain \(y\), and the third does not contain \(x\), so the corresponding elements of the matrix are 0.

The columns of the augmented matrix are the coefficients of the unknowns \(x\), \(y\) and \(\lambda\), and the last column contains the constant terms (see Topic 3). In the constraint equation the coefficient of \(x\) is 1, so it is written as the first row:

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 0 & 1200 \\ 2 & 0 & -100 & -600 \\ 0 & 4 & -100 & -300 \end{array}\right) \]

The first element of the third row is already 0, so we multiply the first row by \(-2\) and add it to the second row only. We divide the new second row by \(-2\):

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 0 & 1200 \\ 2 & 0 & -100 & -600 \\ 0 & 4 & -100 & -300 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \htmlClass{ha}{(-2)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \\ \phantom{0} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1200 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -2 & -100 & -3000 \\ 0 & 4 & -100 & -300 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,(-2)} \\ \phantom{0} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1200 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 50 & 1500 \\ 0 & 4 & -100 & -300 \end{array}\right)\)

We multiply the second row by \(-4\) and add it to the third row, and divide the new third row by \(-300\):

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1200 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 50 & 1500 \\ 0 & 4 & -100 & -300 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{(-4)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1200 \\ 0 & 1 & 50 & 1500 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -300 & -6300 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,(-300)} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1200 \\ 0 & 1 & 50 & 1500 \\ 0 & 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 21 \end{array}\right)\)

Back substitution. The third row gives \(\lambda = 21\). The second row stands for the equation \(y + 50\lambda = 1500\), and the first for \(x + y = 1200\):

\[ y + 50\cdot 21 = 1500 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 450;\qquad x + 450 = 1200 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 750 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} y + 50\cdot 21 = 1500 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 450 \\ x + 450 = 1200 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 750 \end{gathered} \]

The same solution is obtained: \(x = 750\), \(y = 450\), \(\lambda = 21\).

The second order partial derivatives of the Lagrangian function are constant and do not depend on \(\lambda\), so their values at the critical point \(K\) are the same numbers:

\[ \begin{gathered} A = L''_{xx}(K) = 0.02;\quad B = L''_{xy}(K) = 0;\quad C = L''_{yy}(K) = 0.04 \\ D = AC - B^2 = 0.02\cdot 0.04 - 0^2 = 0.0008 > 0 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = 0.02 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = 0.04 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= 0.02\cdot 0.04 - 0^2 \\ &= 0.0008 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A > 0\), so \(K(750;\,450)\) is a minimum point.

7The minimum cost and its change

a) We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the cost function:

\[ \begin{aligned} TC_{\min}(750;\,450) &= 0.01\cdot 750^2 + 6\cdot 750 + 0.02\cdot 450^2 + 3\cdot 450 \\ &= 5625 + 4500 + 4050 + 1350 = 15\,525 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &TC_{\min}(750;\,450) \\ &= 0.01\cdot 750^2 + 6\cdot 750 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 0.02\cdot 450^2 + 3\cdot 450 \\ &= 5625 + 4500 + 4050 + 1350 \\ &= 15\,525 \end{aligned} \]

b) The amount delivered increases by 10 %, i.e. the constraint constant increases by \(\Delta m = 0.1\cdot 1200 = 120\) pallets. The Lagrange multiplier is \(\lambda = 21\), so (see the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier):

\[ \Delta TC^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m = 21\cdot 120 = 2520 \]
Answer

a) 750 pallets should be delivered from warehouse A and 450 from warehouse B. The minimum cost is 15 525 euros a week. b) The minimum cost would increase by approximately 2520 euros.

Example 4

Workers and machines in a small workshop

Problem A workshop with \(x\) workers and \(y\) machines produces \[ Q(x;\,y) = 30x + 40y + xy - x^2 - 2y^2 \] units a day. The workshop has room for exactly 34 workplaces. Each workplace is taken either by one worker or by one machine, so the number of workers and the number of machines together must be 34.
a) How many workers and machines maximize the output? What is the maximum output?
b) What is the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier in this problem?

1The model

The variables: \(x\) is the number of workers, and \(y\) is the number of machines.

The objective function. The maximum output has to be found. The production function \(Q(x;\,y)\) is given in the problem.

The constraint. Each workplace is taken by one worker or by one machine, so the number of workers and the number of machines add up to the number of workplaces: \(x + y = 34\).

We look for the maximum output with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; Q(x;\,y) = 30x + 40y + xy - x^2 - 2y^2 \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 34 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; Q(x;\,y) \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 34 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We write the constraint in the form \(x + y - 34 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = 30x + 40y + xy - x^2 - 2y^2 - \lambda(x + y - 34) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= 30x + 40y + xy - x^2 - 2y^2 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(x + y - 34) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

The term \(xy\) depends on both variables: \((xy)'_x = y\), \((xy)'_y = x\). The derivative of the term \(-\lambda(x + y - 34)\) with respect to each variable is \(-\lambda\):

\[ L'_x = 30 + y - 2x - \lambda;\qquad L'_y = 40 + x - 4y - \lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 30 + y - 2x - \lambda \\ L'_y = 40 + x - 4y - \lambda \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 30 + y - 2x - \lambda &= 0 \\ 40 + x - 4y - \lambda &= 0 \\ x + y &= 34 \end{aligned} \right. \]

All the unknowns are of the first degree, so the system is linear.

4Solving the system by substitution

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first two equations we move the term \(-\lambda\) to the right-hand side:

\[ \lambda = 30 + y - 2x;\qquad \lambda = 40 + x - 4y \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = 30 + y - 2x \\ \lambda = 40 + x - 4y \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the two expressions equal. We move the terms with the variables to the left-hand side and the numbers to the right-hand side, and collect like terms:

\[ 30 + y - 2x = 40 + x - 4y \;\Longrightarrow\; y + 4y - 2x - x = 40 - 30 \;\Longrightarrow\; 5y - 3x = 10 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 30 + y - 2x = 40 + x - 4y \\ \Longrightarrow\; y + 4y - 2x - x = 40 - 30 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 5y - 3x = 10 \end{gathered} \]

Substitution. From the constraint equation \(x + y = 34\) we express \(y = 34 - x\) and substitute it into the equation obtained. Since \(y\) is multiplied by 5, the substituted expression is written in brackets:

\[ 5(34 - x) - 3x = 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; 170 - 5x - 3x = 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; -8x = -160 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 20 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 5(34 - x) - 3x = 10 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 170 - 5x - 3x = 10 \\ \Longrightarrow\; -8x = -160 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 20 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(y = 34 - 20 = 14\).

The value of the multiplier. We substitute the values into both expressions for \(\lambda\) – the same value is obtained:

\[ \lambda = 30 + 14 - 2\cdot 20 = 4;\qquad \lambda = 40 + 20 - 4\cdot 14 = 4 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = 30 + 14 - 2\cdot 20 = 4 \\ \lambda = 40 + 20 - 4\cdot 14 = 4 \end{gathered} \]

We obtain the critical point \(K(20;\,14)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 4\).

This system is linear, so it can also be solved by Gaussian elimination. If you prefer this method, the solution is given below.

First we rearrange the equations: the unknowns are written on the left-hand side in the order \(x\), \(y\), \(\lambda\), and the numbers are moved to the right-hand side:

The same solution is obtained: \(x = 20\), \(y = 14\), \(\lambda = 4\).

We look for the maximum utility with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; U(x;\,y) = 6\sqrt{x} + 4\sqrt{y} \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; 8x + 2y = 200 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We write the constraint in the form \(8x + 2y - 200 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = 6\sqrt{x} + 4\sqrt{y} - \lambda(8x + 2y - 200) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= 6\sqrt{x} + 4\sqrt{y} \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(8x + 2y - 200) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

We use the formula \(\big(\sqrt{x}\big)'_x = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\): \(\big(6\sqrt{x}\big)'_x = \frac{6}{2\sqrt{x}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{x}}\), \(\big(4\sqrt{y}\big)'_y = \frac{4}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{y}}\). After expanding the brackets in the last term we get \(-8\lambda x - 2\lambda y + 200\lambda\), so its derivative with respect to \(x\) is \(-8\lambda\), and with respect to \(y\) – \(-2\lambda\):

\[ L'_x = \frac{3}{\sqrt{x}} - 8\lambda;\qquad L'_y = \frac{2}{\sqrt{y}} - 2\lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = \frac{3}{\sqrt{x}} - 8\lambda \\ L'_y = \frac{2}{\sqrt{y}} - 2\lambda \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} \frac{3}{\sqrt{x}} - 8\lambda &= 0 \\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{y}} - 2\lambda &= 0 \\ 8x + 2y &= 200 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables \(x\) and \(y\) are under roots in the denominators of fractions. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

The variables are the numbers of tickets and films, and they are under roots in denominators, so \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\).

4Solving the system

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first equation we move the term \(-8\lambda\) to the right-hand side and divide both sides by 8. In the second equation we move the term \(-2\lambda\) to the right-hand side and divide both sides by 2:

\[ \lambda = \frac{3}{8\sqrt{x}};\qquad \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{y}} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = \frac{3}{8\sqrt{x}} \\ \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{y}} \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the two expressions equal. Two fractions are equal, so their cross products are also equal: the numerator of the left fraction multiplied by the denominator of the right one is equal to the numerator of the right fraction multiplied by the denominator of the left one:

\[ \frac{3}{8\sqrt{x}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{y}} \;\Longrightarrow\; 3\sqrt{y} = 8\sqrt{x} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{3}{8\sqrt{x}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{y}} \\ \Longrightarrow\; 3\sqrt{y} = 8\sqrt{x} \end{gathered} \]

To get rid of the roots, we square both sides: \(\big(3\sqrt{y}\big)^2 = 9y\), \(\big(8\sqrt{x}\big)^2 = 64x\). Both sides of the equation are positive, so squaring does not produce extraneous solutions. Then we divide both sides by 9 and express \(y\):

\[ 9y = 64x \;\Longrightarrow\; y = \frac{64x}{9} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 9y = 64x \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = \frac{64x}{9} \end{gathered} \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression into the constraint equation \(8x + 2y = 200\) in place of \(y\). To remove the fraction, we multiply both sides of the equation by 9:

\[ \begin{gathered} 8x + 2\cdot\frac{64x}{9} = 200 \;\Longrightarrow\; 8x + \frac{128x}{9} = 200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 72x + 128x = 1800 \;\Longrightarrow\; 200x = 1800 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 9 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 8x + 2\cdot\frac{64x}{9} = 200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 8x + \frac{128x}{9} = 200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 72x + 128x = 1800 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 200x = 1800 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 9 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(y = \frac{64\cdot 9}{9} = 64\).

The value of the multiplier. We substitute \(y = 64\) into the second expression for \(\lambda\) and check with the first (\(\sqrt{64} = 8\), \(\sqrt{9} = 3\)):

\[ \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{64}} = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125;\qquad \lambda = \frac{3}{8\cdot\sqrt{9}} = \frac{3}{24} = 0.125 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{64}} = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125 \\ \lambda = \frac{3}{8\cdot\sqrt{9}} = \frac{3}{24} = 0.125 \end{gathered} \]

We obtain the critical point \(K(9;\,64)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 0.125\).

5The sufficient condition

To make the second differentiation easier, we write the fractions as powers: \(\frac{3}{\sqrt{x}} = 3x^{-0.5}\), \(\frac{2}{\sqrt{y}} = 2y^{-0.5}\). The terms with \(\lambda\) in the first order derivatives are constant, so \(\lambda\) does not remain in the second order derivatives:

\[ L''_{xx} = -1.5x^{-1.5};\qquad L''_{xy} = 0;\qquad L''_{yy} = -y^{-1.5} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L''_{xx} = -1.5x^{-1.5} \\ L''_{xy} = 0 \\ L''_{yy} = -y^{-1.5} \end{gathered} \]

The derivatives contain variables, so we substitute the coordinates of the critical point \(K(9;\,64)\). At this point \(x^{1.5} = 9\cdot\sqrt{9} = 27\), and \(y^{1.5} = 64\cdot\sqrt{64} = 512\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -\frac{1.5}{9^{1.5}} = -\frac{1.5}{27} = -\frac{1}{18} \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -\frac{1}{64^{1.5}} = -\frac{1}{512} \\ D &= AC - B^2 = \Big(-\frac{1}{18}\Big)\cdot\Big(-\frac{1}{512}\Big) - 0^2 = \frac{1}{9216} > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -\frac{1.5}{9^{1.5}} \\ &= -\frac{1.5}{27} = -\frac{1}{18} \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -\frac{1}{64^{1.5}} \\ &= -\frac{1}{512} \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= \Big(-\frac{1}{18}\Big)\cdot\Big(-\frac{1}{512}\Big) - 0^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{9216} > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K(9;\,64)\) is a maximum point.

6The maximum utility and its change

a) We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the utility function:

\[ U_{\max}(9;\,64) = 6\sqrt{9} + 4\sqrt{64} = 6\cdot 3 + 4\cdot 8 = 50 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} U_{\max}(9;\,64) &= 6\sqrt{9} + 4\sqrt{64} \\ &= 6\cdot 3 + 4\cdot 8 = 50 \end{aligned} \]

b) The budget increases by 20 euros, i.e. \(\Delta m = 20\). The Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 0.125\) shows that when the budget increases by one euro, the maximum utility increases by approximately 0.125 units (see the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier):

\[ \Delta U^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m = 0.125\cdot 20 = 2.5 \]
Answer

a) The student should buy 9 cinema tickets and rent 64 films a month. The maximum utility is 50. b) The maximum utility would increase by approximately 2.5 units.

Example 6

Labour and machine hours within a budget

Problem An assembly plant produces \[ Q(x;\,y) = 20x^{0.75}y^{0.25} \] units a day, where \(x\) is the number of hours of labour and \(y\) is the number of machine hours. An hour of labour costs 15 euros, a machine hour costs 80 euros, and the daily budget for both inputs is 3200 euros.
a) How many hours of labour and machine hours maximize the output? What is the maximum output?
b) How would the maximum output change if the budget increased by 100 euros?

1The model

The variables: \(x\) is the number of hours of labour a day, and \(y\) is the number of machine hours a day.

The objective function. The maximum output has to be found. The production function – the Cobb-Douglas function \(Q(x;\,y)\) – is given in the problem.

The constraint is built from the budget. The hours of labour cost \(15x\) euros, and the machine hours cost \(80y\) euros. Together these amounts must equal the daily budget: \(15x + 80y = 3200\).

We look for the maximum output with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; Q(x;\,y) = 20x^{0.75}y^{0.25} \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; 15x + 80y = 3200 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We write the constraint in the form \(15x + 80y - 3200 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = 20x^{0.75}y^{0.25} - \lambda(15x + 80y - 3200) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) \\ &= 20x^{0.75}y^{0.25} - \lambda(15x + 80y - 3200) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the factor \(y^{0.25}\) is constant: \(\big(20x^{0.75}\big)'_x = 20\cdot 0.75x^{-0.25} = 15x^{-0.25}\). When differentiating with respect to \(y\), the constant factor is \(x^{0.75}\): \(\big(20y^{0.25}\big)'_y = 20\cdot 0.25y^{-0.75} = 5y^{-0.75}\). The derivatives of the constraint term are \(-15\lambda\) and \(-80\lambda\):

\[ L'_x = 15x^{-0.25}y^{0.25} - 15\lambda;\qquad L'_y = 5x^{0.75}y^{-0.75} - 80\lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 15x^{-0.25}y^{0.25} - 15\lambda \\ L'_y = 5x^{0.75}y^{-0.75} - 80\lambda \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 15x^{-0.25}y^{0.25} - 15\lambda &= 0 \\ 5x^{0.75}y^{-0.75} - 80\lambda &= 0 \\ 15x + 80y &= 3200 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables are raised to fractional powers and multiplied by each other. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

4Solving the system by dividing the equations

In the first two equations we move the terms with \(\lambda\) to the right-hand sides:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 15x^{-0.25}y^{0.25} &= 15\lambda \\ 5x^{0.75}y^{-0.75} &= 80\lambda \end{aligned} \right. \]

Dividing the equations. Both equations contain the same variables, only in different powers, and \(\lambda\) is on the right-hand sides. Therefore we divide the first equation by the second: the left-hand side by the left-hand side and the right-hand side by the right-hand side. The left-hand sides are positive, so \(\lambda \ne 0\), and \(\lambda\) cancels. Powers of the same base are divided by subtracting their exponents: \(x^{-0.25 - 0.75} = x^{-1}\), \(y^{0.25 - (-0.75)} = y^{1}\):

\[ \frac{15x^{-0.25}y^{0.25}}{5x^{0.75}y^{-0.75}} = \frac{15\lambda}{80\lambda} \;\Longrightarrow\; 3x^{-1}y = \frac{3}{16} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{3y}{x} = \frac{3}{16} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{15x^{-0.25}y^{0.25}}{5x^{0.75}y^{-0.75}} = \frac{15\lambda}{80\lambda} \\ \Longrightarrow\; 3x^{-1}y = \frac{3}{16} \\ \Longrightarrow\; \frac{3y}{x} = \frac{3}{16} \end{gathered} \]

We divide both sides by 3 and cross-multiply: \(16y = x\), i.e.

\[ x = 16y \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression \(x = 16y\) into the constraint equation \(15x + 80y = 3200\):

\[ 15\cdot 16y + 80y = 3200 \;\Longrightarrow\; 240y + 80y = 3200 \;\Longrightarrow\; 320y = 3200 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 10 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 15\cdot 16y + 80y = 3200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 240y + 80y = 3200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 320y = 3200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 10 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(x = 16\cdot 10 = 160\).

The value of the multiplier. Dividing the first equation \(15x^{-0.25}y^{0.25} = 15\lambda\) by 15, we get \(\lambda = x^{-0.25}y^{0.25}\). It is convenient to write this expression as a single fraction: \(x^{-0.25}y^{0.25} = \big(\frac{y}{x}\big)^{0.25}\):

\[ \lambda = \Big(\frac{10}{160}\Big)^{0.25} = \Big(\frac{1}{16}\Big)^{0.25} = 0.5 \]

The last value can be found without a calculator: \(0.5^4 = 0.0625 = \frac{1}{16}\). We obtain the critical point \(K(160;\,10)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 0.5\).

5The type of the critical point

The production function is a Cobb-Douglas function with \(A = 20 > 0\) and \(\alpha + \beta = 0.75 + 0.25 = 1 \le 1\), so it is concave. The budget constraint term is linear, so the Lagrangian function \(L\) is also concave (see Cobb-Douglas functions in constrained optimization problems). Therefore \(K(160;\,10)\) is a maximum point.

6The maximum output and its change

a) We calculate the product of the powers by noticing that \(160 = 16\cdot 10\) and \(16^{0.75} = \big(16^{0.25}\big)^3 = 2^3 = 8\):

\[ 160^{0.75}\cdot 10^{0.25} = 16^{0.75}\cdot 10^{0.75}\cdot 10^{0.25} = 8\cdot 10 = 80 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &160^{0.75}\cdot 10^{0.25} \\ &= 16^{0.75}\cdot 10^{0.75}\cdot 10^{0.25} \\ &= 8\cdot 10 = 80 \end{aligned} \]
\[ Q_{\max}(160;\,10) = 20\cdot 80 = 1600 \]

b) The budget increases by 100 euros, i.e. \(\Delta m = 100\). The Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 0.5\) shows that when the budget increases by one euro, the maximum output increases by approximately 0.5 units (see the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier):

\[ \Delta Q^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m = 0.5\cdot 100 = 50 \]
Answer

a) The plant should use 160 hours of labour and 10 machine hours a day. The maximum output is 1600 units a day. b) The maximum output would increase by approximately 50 units.

Example 7

Printing an order

Problem A printing house prints \[ Q(x;\,y) = 12\sqrt{x} + 18\sqrt{y} \] thousand pages, where \(x\) is the number of hours of the operators' work and \(y\) is the number of machine hours. An operator hour costs 16 euros, and a machine hour costs 36 euros. The printing house has an order for 360 thousand pages.
a) How many operator hours and machine hours minimize the cost of the order? What is the minimum cost?
b) How would the minimum cost change if the order increased by 10 thousand pages?

1The model

The variables: \(x\) is the number of operator hours, and \(y\) is the number of machine hours.

The objective function. The minimum cost of the order has to be found, and the cost function is not given in the problem – we build it. An operator hour costs 16 euros, so \(x\) hours cost \(16x\) euros. A machine hour costs 36 euros, so \(y\) hours cost \(36y\) euros. The cost is the sum of these amounts:

\[ TC(x;\,y) = 16x + 36y \]

The constraint. The function \(Q(x;\,y)\) shows how many thousand pages are printed. The number of pages printed must meet the order of 360 thousand pages: \(12\sqrt{x} + 18\sqrt{y} = 360\). Note that here the production function is not the objective function but the constraint.

We look for the minimum cost with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; TC(x;\,y) = 16x + 36y \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; 12\sqrt{x} + 18\sqrt{y} = 360 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We write the constraint in the form \(12\sqrt{x} + 18\sqrt{y} - 360 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = 16x + 36y - \lambda\big(12\sqrt{x} + 18\sqrt{y} - 360\big) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) \\ &= 16x + 36y \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda\big(12\sqrt{x} + 18\sqrt{y} - 360\big) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

Since \(\big(\sqrt{x}\big)'_x = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\), the derivative of the term \(-12\lambda\sqrt{x}\) with respect to \(x\) is \(-\frac{12\lambda}{2\sqrt{x}} = -\frac{6\lambda}{\sqrt{x}}\). In the same way, the derivative of the term \(-18\lambda\sqrt{y}\) with respect to \(y\) is \(-\frac{9\lambda}{\sqrt{y}}\):

\[ L'_x = 16 - \frac{6\lambda}{\sqrt{x}};\qquad L'_y = 36 - \frac{9\lambda}{\sqrt{y}} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 16 - \frac{6\lambda}{\sqrt{x}} \\ L'_y = 36 - \frac{9\lambda}{\sqrt{y}} \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 16 - \frac{6\lambda}{\sqrt{x}} &= 0 \\ 36 - \frac{9\lambda}{\sqrt{y}} &= 0 \\ 12\sqrt{x} + 18\sqrt{y} &= 360 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables are under roots, and \(\lambda\) is divided by \(\sqrt{x}\) and \(\sqrt{y}\). Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

4Solving the system

This time it is convenient not to set the expressions for \(\lambda\) equal, but to express the roots \(\sqrt{x}\) and \(\sqrt{y}\) through \(\lambda\) and substitute them into the constraint equation, because it contains the same roots.

The roots through \(\lambda\). In the first equation we move the fraction to the right-hand side, multiply both sides by \(\sqrt{x}\) and divide them by 16. The second equation is rearranged in the same way:

\[ \begin{aligned} 16 = \frac{6\lambda}{\sqrt{x}} \;&\Longrightarrow\; 16\sqrt{x} = 6\lambda \;\Longrightarrow\; \sqrt{x} = \frac{6\lambda}{16} = 0.375\lambda \\ 36 = \frac{9\lambda}{\sqrt{y}} \;&\Longrightarrow\; 36\sqrt{y} = 9\lambda \;\Longrightarrow\; \sqrt{y} = \frac{9\lambda}{36} = 0.25\lambda \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 16 = \frac{6\lambda}{\sqrt{x}} \;\Longrightarrow\; 16\sqrt{x} = 6\lambda \\ \Longrightarrow\; \sqrt{x} = \frac{6\lambda}{16} = 0.375\lambda \\[6pt] 36 = \frac{9\lambda}{\sqrt{y}} \;\Longrightarrow\; 36\sqrt{y} = 9\lambda \\ \Longrightarrow\; \sqrt{y} = \frac{9\lambda}{36} = 0.25\lambda \end{gathered} \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expressions obtained into the constraint equation \(12\sqrt{x} + 18\sqrt{y} = 360\) – this gives an equation in one unknown \(\lambda\):

\[ \begin{gathered} 12\cdot 0.375\lambda + 18\cdot 0.25\lambda = 360 \;\Longrightarrow\; 4.5\lambda + 4.5\lambda = 360 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 9\lambda = 360 \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda = 40 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 12\cdot 0.375\lambda + 18\cdot 0.25\lambda = 360 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 4.5\lambda + 4.5\lambda = 360 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 9\lambda = 360 \\ \Longrightarrow\; \lambda = 40 \end{gathered} \]

The values of the variables. We substitute the value \(\lambda = 40\) into the expressions for the roots, and then square both sides of each equality:

\[ \sqrt{x} = 0.375\cdot 40 = 15 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 225;\qquad \sqrt{y} = 0.25\cdot 40 = 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 100 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \sqrt{x} = 0.375\cdot 40 = 15 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 225 \\ \sqrt{y} = 0.25\cdot 40 = 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 100 \end{gathered} \]

We obtain the critical point \(K(225;\,100)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 40\).

5The sufficient condition

We write the fractions as powers: \(\frac{6\lambda}{\sqrt{x}} = 6\lambda x^{-0.5}\), \(\frac{9\lambda}{\sqrt{y}} = 9\lambda y^{-0.5}\). We differentiate once more, for example \(\big(-6\lambda x^{-0.5}\big)'_x = 3\lambda x^{-1.5}\):

\[ L''_{xx} = 3\lambda x^{-1.5};\qquad L''_{xy} = 0;\qquad L''_{yy} = 4.5\lambda y^{-1.5} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L''_{xx} = 3\lambda x^{-1.5} \\ L''_{xy} = 0 \\ L''_{yy} = 4.5\lambda y^{-1.5} \end{gathered} \]

This time the second order derivatives contain not only \(x\) and \(y\) but also \(\lambda\). Therefore we substitute the coordinates of the critical point \(K(225;\,100)\) and the value of the multiplier \(\lambda = 40\). At this point \(x^{1.5} = 225\cdot\sqrt{225} = 3375\), and \(y^{1.5} = 100\cdot\sqrt{100} = 1000\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = \frac{3\cdot 40}{225^{1.5}} = \frac{120}{3375} \approx 0.036 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = \frac{4.5\cdot 40}{100^{1.5}} = \frac{180}{1000} = 0.18 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = \frac{120}{3375}\cdot 0.18 - 0^2 = 0.0064 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = \frac{3\cdot 40}{225^{1.5}} \\ &= \frac{120}{3375} \approx 0.036 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = \frac{4.5\cdot 40}{100^{1.5}} \\ &= \frac{180}{1000} = 0.18 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= \frac{120}{3375}\cdot 0.18 - 0^2 \\ &= 0.0064 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A > 0\), so \(K(225;\,100)\) is a minimum point.

6The minimum cost and its change

a) We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the cost function:

\[ TC_{\min}(225;\,100) = 16\cdot 225 + 36\cdot 100 = 3600 + 3600 = 7200 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} TC_{\min}(225;\,100) &= 16\cdot 225 + 36\cdot 100 \\ &= 3600 + 3600 = 7200 \end{aligned} \]

b) The constraint constant is measured in thousands of pages, so when the order increases by 10 thousand pages, \(\Delta m = 10\). The Lagrange multiplier is \(\lambda = 40\) (see the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier):

\[ \Delta TC^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m = 40\cdot 10 = 400 \]
Answer

a) The order needs 225 operator hours and 100 machine hours. The minimum cost is 7200 euros. b) The minimum cost would increase by approximately 400 euros.

Example 8

An order for a furniture factory

Problem A furniture factory produces \[ Q(x;\,y) = 8x^{0.5}y^{0.5} \] chairs, where \(x\) is the number of hours of labour and \(y\) is the number of machine hours. An hour of labour costs 16 euros, and a machine hour costs 100 euros. The factory has to fill an order for 400 chairs.
a) How many hours of labour and machine hours minimize the cost of the order? What is the minimum cost?
b) How would the minimum cost change if the order increased by 10 %?

1The model

The variables: \(x\) is the number of hours of labour, and \(y\) is the number of machine hours.

The objective function. The minimum cost of the order has to be found. Labour costs \(16x\) euros, and the use of the machines \(100y\) euros, so the cost function is

\[ TC(x;\,y) = 16x + 100y \]

The constraint. The number of chairs produced must meet the order: \(8x^{0.5}y^{0.5} = 400\). As in the previous example, the production function here is the constraint. Compare with Example 6, in which the Cobb-Douglas function was the objective function and the constraint was set by the budget.

We look for the minimum cost with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; TC(x;\,y) = 16x + 100y \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; 8x^{0.5}y^{0.5} = 400 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We write the constraint in the form \(8x^{0.5}y^{0.5} - 400 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = 16x + 100y - \lambda\big(8x^{0.5}y^{0.5} - 400\big) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= 16x + 100y \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda\big(8x^{0.5}y^{0.5} - 400\big) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

The derivative of the term \(-8\lambda x^{0.5}y^{0.5}\) with respect to \(x\) is \(-8\lambda\cdot 0.5x^{-0.5}y^{0.5} = -4\lambda x^{-0.5}y^{0.5}\), and with respect to \(y\) – \(-4\lambda x^{0.5}y^{-0.5}\):

\[ L'_x = 16 - 4\lambda x^{-0.5}y^{0.5};\qquad L'_y = 100 - 4\lambda x^{0.5}y^{-0.5} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 16 - 4\lambda x^{-0.5}y^{0.5} \\ L'_y = 100 - 4\lambda x^{0.5}y^{-0.5} \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 16 - 4\lambda x^{-0.5}y^{0.5} &= 0 \\ 100 - 4\lambda x^{0.5}y^{-0.5} &= 0 \\ 8x^{0.5}y^{0.5} &= 400 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables are raised to powers and multiplied by each other and by \(\lambda\). Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

4Solving the system by dividing the equations

In the first two equations we move the terms with \(\lambda\) to the right-hand sides and write the equalities the other way round:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 4\lambda x^{-0.5}y^{0.5} &= 16 \\ 4\lambda x^{0.5}y^{-0.5} &= 100 \end{aligned} \right. \]

Dividing the equations. We divide the first equation by the second. The factor \(4\lambda\) cancels, and the exponents are subtracted: \(x^{-0.5 - 0.5} = x^{-1}\), \(y^{0.5 - (-0.5)} = y\):

\[ \frac{4\lambda x^{-0.5}y^{0.5}}{4\lambda x^{0.5}y^{-0.5}} = \frac{16}{100} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{y}{x} = 0.16 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 0.16x \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{4\lambda x^{-0.5}y^{0.5}}{4\lambda x^{0.5}y^{-0.5}} = \frac{16}{100} \\ \Longrightarrow\; \frac{y}{x} = 0.16 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 0.16x \end{gathered} \]

Substituting into the constraint. Since \(x^{0.5}y^{0.5} = \sqrt{xy}\), substituting \(y = 0.16x\) gives \(\sqrt{x\cdot 0.16x} = \sqrt{0.16x^2} = 0.4x\) (because \(x > 0\)):

\[ 8\sqrt{x\cdot 0.16x} = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; 8\cdot 0.4x = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3.2x = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 125 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 8\sqrt{x\cdot 0.16x} = 400 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 8\cdot 0.4x = 400 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 3.2x = 400 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 125 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(y = 0.16\cdot 125 = 20\).

The value of the multiplier. In the first equation \(x^{-0.5}y^{0.5} = \sqrt{\frac{y}{x}} = \sqrt{\frac{20}{125}} = \sqrt{0.16} = 0.4\):

\[ 4\lambda\cdot 0.4 = 16 \;\Longrightarrow\; 1.6\lambda = 16 \;\Longrightarrow\; \lambda = 10 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 4\lambda\cdot 0.4 = 16 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 1.6\lambda = 16 \\ \Longrightarrow\; \lambda = 10 \end{gathered} \]

We obtain the critical point \(K(125;\,20)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 10\).

5The type of the critical point

The left-hand side of the constraint is a Cobb-Douglas function with \(A = 8 > 0\) and \(\alpha + \beta = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 \le 1\), so it is concave. Since \(\lambda = 10 > 0\), the term \(-\lambda\big(8x^{0.5}y^{0.5} - 400\big)\) is convex. The cost function is linear, so \(L\) is a convex function (see Cobb-Douglas functions in constrained optimization problems), and \(K(125;\,20)\) is a minimum point.

6The minimum cost and its change

a) We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the cost function:

\[ TC_{\min}(125;\,20) = 16\cdot 125 + 100\cdot 20 = 2000 + 2000 = 4000 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} TC_{\min}(125;\,20) &= 16\cdot 125 + 100\cdot 20 \\ &= 2000 + 2000 = 4000 \end{aligned} \]

b) The order increases by 10 %, i.e. \(\Delta m = 0.1\cdot 400 = 40\) chairs. The Lagrange multiplier is \(\lambda = 10\) (see the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier):

\[ \Delta TC^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m = 10\cdot 40 = 400 \]
Answer

a) The order needs 125 hours of labour and 20 machine hours. The minimum cost is 4000 euros. b) The minimum cost would increase by approximately 400 euros.
