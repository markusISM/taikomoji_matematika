Rational Planning: Infinitely Many Solutions

This section deals with planning problems whose system of equations has infinitely many solutions: how to write the solutions with a parameter and select the plans that make business sense.

  • Model
  • Many solutions
  • Examples
  • Practice

Building the model: a short reminder

The system of equations is built in the same way as when the solution is unique:

1
VariablesOne variable for each quantity to be decided, defined in a sentence with its unit.
2
EquationsOne equation “used = available” for each resource or requirement.
3
Gaussian eliminationThe system is written as an augmented matrix and transformed into triangular form.

Building the model in more detail – in Section 1 →

When there are infinitely many solutions and what to do

A planning problem has infinitely many solutions when there are fewer conditions than quantities to be found: for example, the quantities of three kinds of product have to be found, but there are only two requirements. This also happens when one of the equations gives no new information – Gaussian elimination turns it into a row of zeros.

In both cases fewer non-zero rows than variables remain when forward elimination is finished. Every variable that has no pivot element left is free – it can take any value (see Topic 3).

What to do

  1. Free variables. Their number is the number of variables minus the number of non-zero rows.
  2. Parameter. Set the variable without a leading 1 equal to a parameter: \(z = t\).
  3. The other variables through the parameter. Express the remaining variables through \(t\) by back substitution.
  4. Business sense. Mathematically every value of \(t\) gives a solution, but quantities cannot be negative, and things counted in pieces cannot be fractional. Write the inequality “\(\geq 0\)” for every variable and find from them which values \(t\) can take.
  5. Possible plans. Each admissible value of \(t\) gives one plan (a particular solution). Count the plans, list them in a table and answer any further questions of the problem.

All the plans obtained satisfy the conditions of the problem equally well, so the arithmetic alone cannot choose between them. The choice is a managerial one, made on grounds that are not in the model – for example, the purchase price or running costs. The coefficients of the parameter show the trade-off: how much the other quantities change when \(t\) increases by one.

!Common mistakes
  • The sign when dividing by a negative number. When an inequality is divided by a negative number, its sign is reversed.
  • The parameter forgotten. Not only the expressed variables but also the free variable itself must be non-negative: \(t \geq 0\).
  • A fractional bound. If, for example, \(t \leq 18\tfrac{1}{3}\) is obtained, the whole-number bound is \(t \leq 18\).
  • The parameter for the wrong variable. The parameter stands for the variable that has no pivot element left; the others are expressed through it.
  • Only one plan given. The answer must contain the general solution with the interval of the parameter values, and specific plans if they are asked for.
What you must write in the exam
  1. When solving applied systems of linear equations problems, the definitions of the introduced variables must be provided at the beginning of the solution. Each variable definition must clearly state that the variable represents the quantity of a specific object.
  2. If convenient for the author, the data from the problem may be summarized in a table, but this is optional.
  3. The solution must include the mathematical model of the problem – the system of linear equations. Presenting only the augmented matrix is not sufficient.
  4. The system of equations must be solved using the Gaussian elimination method. The solution must show which row operations are performed to eliminate elements below the main diagonal.
  5. At the end of the solution, the final answer must be clearly presented.
  6. If the system has infinitely many solutions, they must be expressed using a parameter. In applied problems, it is necessary to indicate the interval of possible parameter values. If required by the problem, particular solutions must also be provided.

Example 1

A purchase plan for freezers

Problem

A supermarket chain wants to purchase 20 display freezers with a combined capacity of 4800 litres. Three different models are available: a compact model with a capacity of 150 litres, a standard model with a capacity of 300 litres, and a large model with a capacity of 600 litres. How many freezers of each model should the chain purchase?

1Decision variables

  • \(x\)– the number of compact freezers purchased;
  • \(y\)– the number of standard freezers purchased;
  • \(z\)– the number of large freezers purchased.

2The system of equations

There are two requirements, so there are only two equations for three variables: one equation counts the freezers and one counts the litres.

In the problemEquation
20 freezers altogether\(x + y + z = 20\)
A combined capacity of 4800 litres\(150x + 300y + 600z = 4800\)

Together the two equations make up the mathematical model of the problem – the system of equations:

\[ \begin{cases} x + y + z = 20 \\ 150x + 300y + 600z = 4800 \end{cases} \]

There are fewer equations than variables, so there cannot be a unique solution.

3Gaussian elimination

Dividing the second equation by 150 gives \(x + 2y + 4z = 32\). Then the first row is multiplied by \(-1\) and added to the second:

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 1 & 20 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 & 32 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \htmlClass{ha}{(-1)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 20 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 3 & 12 \end{array}\right)\)

4The parameter

There are 3 variables and 2 non-zero rows, so there is one free variable: \(3 - 2 = 1\). The variable \(z\) has no pivot element left, so we take \(z = t\) and express the other variables by back substitution:

From the second row \(y + 3t = 12\), from the first \(x + y + t = 20\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= 12 - 3t \\ x &= 20 - (12 - 3t) - t = 8 + 2t \end{aligned} \]

5Values of the parameter

The numbers of freezers must be whole numbers that are not negative:

\[ \begin{aligned} x \geq 0:&\quad 8 + 2t \geq 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t \geq -4 \\ y \geq 0:&\quad 12 - 3t \geq 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t \leq 4 \\ z \geq 0:&\quad t \geq 0 \end{aligned} \]

All three conditions together: \(t \in [0,\, 4] \cap \mathbb{Z}\), i.e. \(t\) can be 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4.

6The possible plans

\(t\)01234
Compact \(x\)810121416
Standard \(y\)129630
Large \(z\)01234

Every plan contains 20 freezers and 4800 litres. For instance, for \(t = 2\): \(12 + 6 + 2 = 20\) and \(150\cdot 12 + 300\cdot 6 + 600\cdot 2 = 4800\).

Answer \[ \begin{gathered} (x;\, y;\, z) = (8 + 2t;\; 12 - 3t;\; t), \\ t \in [0,\, 4] \cap \mathbb{Z} \end{gathered} \]

There are five possible purchases: 8 compact and 12 standard freezers; 10 compact, 9 standard and 1 large; 12 compact, 6 standard and 2 large; 14 compact, 3 standard and 3 large; or 16 compact and 4 large freezers.

The coefficients of the parameter show the trade-off: every extra large freezer means two more compact freezers and three fewer standard ones. The chain decides which option to choose on price or running costs.

Example 2

Coffee machines with an extra requirement

Problem

A hotel chain wants to buy 30 coffee machines with a combined capacity of 1000 cups per hour. Three models are available, with capacities of 20, 40 and 60 cups per hour. How many machines of each model can be bought? The chain also insists on having at least 8 machines of the largest model; which plans satisfy this?

1Decision variables

Let \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) be the numbers of machines of 20, 40 and 60 cups per hour.

2The system of equations

Two requirements – the number of machines and the total capacity – give two equations for three unknowns:

\[ \begin{cases} x + y + z = 30 \\ 20x + 40y + 60z = 1000 \end{cases} \]

The requirement “at least 8 machines of the largest model” is an inequality, not an equation, so it will be checked later, when all the plans are known.

3Gaussian elimination

Dividing the capacity equation by 20 gives \(x + 2y + 3z = 50\). Then \(-1\) times the first row is added to the second:

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 1 & 30 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 50 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \htmlClass{ha}{(-1)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 30 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 2 & 20 \end{array}\right)\)

4The parameter

Two non-zero rows and three variables leave one free variable. The variable without a leading 1 is \(z\), so let \(z = t\):

From the second row \(y + 2t = 20\), from the first \(x + y + t = 30\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= 20 - 2t \\ x &= 30 - (20 - 2t) - t = 10 + t \end{aligned} \]

5Values of the parameter

The numbers of machines must be whole and non-negative. Since \(x = 10 + t\) is positive for every \(t \geq 0\), the only binding condition is

\[ y \geq 0:\quad 20 - 2t \geq 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t \leq 10 \]

So \(t \in [0,\, 10] \cap \mathbb{Z}\) – eleven different purchase plans.

6The extra requirement

The requirement \(z \geq 8\) means \(t \geq 8\), so only three of the eleven plans remain:

\(t = z\)8910
\(x\)181920
\(y\)420
\(z\)8910

Check one of them, \((19;\, 2;\, 9)\): \(19 + 2 + 9 = 30\) machines and

\[ 20\cdot 19 + 40\cdot 2 + 60\cdot 9 = 1000 \]

cups per hour.

Answer

There are eleven possible plans:

\[ \begin{gathered} (x;\, y;\, z) = (10 + t;\; 20 - 2t;\; t), \\ t \in [0,\, 10] \cap \mathbb{Z} \end{gathered} \]

With at least 8 machines of the largest model, three remain: \((18;\, 4;\, 8)\), \((19;\, 2;\, 9)\) and \((20;\, 0;\, 10)\).

All of them meet the requirements exactly, so the chain can choose on price or reliability grounds.

Example 3

A production plan for a packaging workshop

Problem

A packaging workshop makes four products: boxes, crates, pallets and trays. One box takes 2 labour-hours, 1 machine-hour and 3 kg of material; one crate takes 1 labour-hour, 2 machine-hours and 1 kg; one pallet takes 3 labour-hours, 1 machine-hour and 2 kg; one tray takes 4 labour-hours, 5 machine-hours and 5 kg. In a shift 100 labour-hours, 90 machine-hours and 110 kg of material are available. Find all production plans that use every resource exactly.

1Decision variables

Let \(x_1\), \(x_2\), \(x_3\) and \(x_4\) be the numbers of boxes, crates, pallets and trays made in the shift.

2The table and the system of equations

The data, with one row per resource and one column per product (\(x_1\) – boxes, \(x_2\) – crates, \(x_3\) – pallets, \(x_4\) – trays):

Boxes \(x_1\)Crates \(x_2\)Pallets \(x_3\)Trays \(x_4\)Available
Labour, h2134100
Machine, h121590
Material, kg3125110
\[ \begin{cases} 2x_1 + x_2 + 3x_3 + 4x_4 = 100 \\ x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 + 5x_4 = 90 \\ 3x_1 + x_2 + 2x_3 + 5x_4 = 110 \end{cases} \]

Three equations and four unknowns, so the system cannot have a single solution.

3Zeros in the first column

Interchange the first two rows to obtain a leading 1, and clear the first column below it:

\(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 2 & 1 & 5 & 90 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 & 4 & 100 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 & 5 & 110 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{cc} \htmlClass{ha}{(-2)} & \htmlClass{ha}{(-3)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} & \htmlClass{ha}{\vert} \\ & \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 5 & 90 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -3 & 1 & -6 & -80 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -5 & -1 & -10 & -160 \end{array}\right)\)

4The second leading 1

The third row divides evenly by \(-5\), so rows 2 and 3 are interchanged before dividing:

\(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 5 & 90 \\ 0 & -5 & -1 & -10 & -160 \\ 0 & -3 & 1 & -6 & -80 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,(-5)} \\ \phantom{0} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 5 & 90 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 0.2 & 2 & 32 \\ 0 & -3 & 1 & -6 & -80 \end{array}\right)\)

5A zero in the second column

Add 3 times the second row to the third, then divide the result by 1.6:

\(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 5 & 90 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 0.2 & 2 & 32 \\ 0 & -3 & 1 & -6 & -80 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{(3)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 5 & 90 \\ 0 & 1 & 0.2 & 2 & 32 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & 1.6 & 0 & 16 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,1.6} \end{array}\) \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\left(\begin{array}{cccc|c} 1 & 2 & 1 & 5 & 90 \\ 0 & 1 & 0.2 & 2 & 32 \\ 0 & 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 0 & 10 \end{array}\right)\)

6The parameter

There are three non-zero rows and four variables, so one variable is free. The last row reads \(x_3 = 10\), and \(x_4\) has no pivot element anywhere, so it is the free one. Put \(x_4 = t\) and substitute upwards:

\[ \begin{aligned} &x_2 + 0.2\cdot 10 + 2t = 32 \\ &\Longrightarrow\; x_2 = 30 - 2t \\[4pt] &x_1 + 2(30 - 2t) + 10 + 5t = 90 \\ &\Longrightarrow\; x_1 = 20 - t \end{aligned} \]

7Values of the parameter

The quantities must be non-negative whole numbers:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1 \geq 0:&\quad 20 - t \geq 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t \leq 20 \\ x_2 \geq 0:&\quad 30 - 2t \geq 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t \leq 15 \\ x_4 \geq 0:&\quad t \geq 0 \end{aligned} \]

So \(t \in [0,\, 15] \cap \mathbb{Z}\) – sixteen plans. Three of them are:

\(t\)0515
\(x_1\)20155
\(x_2\)30200
\(x_3\)101010
\(x_4\)0515

8What the result shows

Two things are worth noticing before choosing. First, 10 pallets appear in every plan: the resources fix that quantity whatever else the workshop does. Second, the parameter shows the trade-off – every extra tray costs one box and two crates.

Answer \[ \begin{gathered} (x_1;\, x_2;\, x_3;\, x_4) = {} \\ (20 - t;\; 30 - 2t;\; 10;\; t), \\ t \in [0,\, 15] \cap \mathbb{Z} \end{gathered} \]

Every plan of this form uses all three resources exactly, so there are sixteen feasible production plans.

Practice problems

Write the solutions out as in the examples: define the variables, build the system of equations, solve it by Gaussian elimination, express the solutions with a parameter and state the interval of its values. The answer gives the variables, the system and the final answer – use them to check your work.

A joinery makes interior doors, front doors and door frames. One interior door takes 4 hours of machine time and 5 boards, one front door takes 5 hours and 6 boards, and one door frame takes 2 hours and 3 boards. Next month the joinery has 240 hours of machine time and 300 boards, and both are to be used exactly. Find all the production plans that are possible, and state how many of them there are.

The variables:

  • \(x\)– the number of interior doors;
  • \(y\)– the number of front doors;
  • \(z\)– the number of door frames.

The system. The first equation counts the hours of machine time and the second the boards:

\[ \begin{cases} 4x + 5y + 2z = 240 \\ 5x + 6y + 3z = 300 \end{cases} \]

Answer. There are three variables and two non-zero rows, so the system has one parameter and infinitely many solutions:

\[ x = 60 - 3t, \quad y = 2t, \quad z = t \]

Only whole and non-negative numbers of items make sense, so \(t \in [0,\, 20] \cap \mathbb{Z}\), which leaves twenty-one production plans, from 60 interior doors and nothing else to 40 front doors and 20 door frames and no interior doors. In every plan the joinery makes twice as many front doors as door frames, and each additional door frame goes together with two additional front doors and three interior doors fewer.

A delicatessen packs four kinds of gift hamper. A classic hamper contains 1 jar of honey, 1 bottle of wine, 2 boxes of chocolate and 3 packs of coffee; a deluxe hamper contains 1 jar of honey, 2 bottles of wine, 3 boxes of chocolate and 4 packs of coffee; a wine lover’s hamper contains 1 jar of honey, 3 bottles of wine, 1 box of chocolate and 2 packs of coffee; and an alcohol-free hamper contains 1 jar of honey, 1 box of chocolate and 2 packs of coffee. The shop has 100 jars of honey, 140 bottles of wine, 150 boxes of chocolate and 250 packs of coffee, and all of them are to be packed into hampers. Find all the ways of packing the hampers, and state how many of them there are.

The variables:

  • \(x\)– the number of classic hampers;
  • \(y\)– the number of deluxe hampers;
  • \(z\)– the number of wine lover’s hampers;
  • \(w\)– the number of alcohol-free hampers.

The system. One equation for each item in stock – the jars of honey, the bottles of wine, the boxes of chocolate and the packs of coffee:

\[ \begin{cases} x + y + z + w = 100 \\ x + 2y + 3z = 140 \\ 2x + 3y + z + w = 150 \\ 3x + 4y + 2z + 2w = 250 \end{cases} \]

Answer. The four equations leave only three non-zero rows – the coffee equation is the sum of the honey equation and the chocolate equation – so with four variables the system has one parameter and infinitely many solutions:

\[ \begin{gathered} x = 90 - 2t, \quad y = t - 20, \\ z = 30, \quad w = t \end{gathered} \]

Only whole and non-negative numbers of hampers make sense, so \(t \in [20,\, 45] \cap \mathbb{Z}\), which leaves twenty-six ways of packing, from \((50;\, 0;\, 30;\, 20)\) to \((0;\, 25;\, 30;\, 45)\). The number of wine lover’s hampers is 30 in every one of them, and every additional alcohol-free hamper goes together with one additional deluxe hamper and two classic hampers fewer.
