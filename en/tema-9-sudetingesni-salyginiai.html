Advanced Constrained Optimization Problems

In the previous section the objective function and the constraint were given, or they could be written directly from prices and costs. In the problems of this section the most important step is building the model: the objective function and the constraint have to be found from the description of the situation, often with the help of geometry. The model is then solved as in the previous section.

Building a constrained optimization model

Three questions help to build the model.

  1. What are the variables? They may be quantities, the coordinates of a point on a map, the dimensions of a figure, amounts of money or lengths of time.
  2. Which quantity has to be maximized or minimized? This gives the objective function \(f(x;\,y)\).
  3. What condition must the variables satisfy? It gives the constraint \(g(x;\,y) = m\). The constraint may be given by the equation of a road or of the boundary of an area, by a production possibilities curve, by a budget made up of income from several sources, or by a total amount of money.

Useful geometric facts

  • The squared distance between the points \((x;\,y)\) and \((a;\,b)\) is \((x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2\). The distance is the square root of this expression. Both quantities take their smallest values at the same point, so it is enough to minimize the squared distance (Examples 2 and 4).
  • A straight road or another line on a map is given by an equation \(ax + by = c\). If a point is on the road, its coordinates satisfy this equation (Example 2).
  • A circle with the radius \(r\) and the centre at the origin has the equation \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) (Example 4).
  • An ellipse with the centre at the origin has an equation of the form \(ax^2 + by^2 = m\). If a rectangle inscribed in the ellipse has its sides parallel to the axes and one corner at \((x;\,y)\), its sides are \(2x\) and \(2y\) long, and its area is \(4xy\) (Example 3).

When the constraint has to be built from several parts

In economic problems the constraint is often obtained by combining several statements of the problem. For example, if 16 hours a day can be spent on work and leisure, and \(l\) is the number of hours of leisure, then \(16 - l\) hours remain for work. Multiplying by the hourly wage and adding the other income, we get the total income, which is equal to the consumption spending (Example 5).

The system of equations, several critical points and the sufficient condition

The model is solved as in the previous section. We build the Lagrangian function \(L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = f(x;\,y) - \lambda\big(g(x;\,y) - m\big)\), set both first order partial derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system. After solving the system of three equations, at each critical point \(K(x_0;\,y_0)\), also substituting the corresponding value \(\lambda_0\) of the multiplier, we calculate the values of the second order derivatives \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) and \(D = AC - B^2\). For a Cobb-Douglas function we can use its concavity (see “Cobb-Douglas functions in constrained optimization problems”).

Methods of solving the system

  • A linear system (the sum of the squared distances and a straight road) is solved by substitution or by Gaussian elimination (Example 2).
  • When the constraint is a curve (a circle, an ellipse, a production possibilities curve), the equations contain the products \(\lambda x\), \(\lambda y\) and squares, so the system is non-linear and Gaussian elimination cannot be used. We express \(\lambda\) from the first two equations, set the expressions equal and get a relation between \(x\) and \(y\). Substituting it into the constraint often gives an incomplete quadratic equation, which we factor (Examples 1, 3 and 4).
  • A Cobb-Douglas function – we move the terms with \(\lambda\) to the right-hand sides and divide one equation by the other (Example 5).
  • Exponential functions – after setting the expressions for \(\lambda\) equal, we take the logarithm of both sides of the equation (Example 6).

An incomplete quadratic equation has two solutions of opposite signs. If the variables are quantities or dimensions, the negative solution is rejected. If the variables are the coordinates of a point on a map, both solutions are suitable.

Several critical points

When the constraint defines a curve, the system may have several solutions. The sufficient condition is checked at each point. One point may be a maximum point and another a minimum point (Example 4).

When the test is not sufficient

If \(D = 0\), the function is examined along the constraint curve. When the admissible curve is an arc with endpoints, we compare the values of the objective function at its endpoints and at the interior critical points (Example 3).

How to solve

  1. Answer the three questions and write the model: the variables, the objective function \(f(x;\,y)\) and the constraint \(g(x;\,y) = m\) (see “Building a constrained optimization model”).
  2. Write down the Lagrangian function \(L = f - \lambda(g - m)\).
  3. Find \(L'_x\) and \(L'_y\), set them equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system. Solve the system of three equations and reject the solutions that have no meaning in the problem. Label the critical points \(K\) or \(K_1\), \(K_2\), and so on, and write the values of \(\lambda\) separately.
  4. Check the sufficient condition at each critical point: \(A = L''_{xx}(K)\), \(B = L''_{xy}(K)\), \(C = L''_{yy}(K)\). If \(D = 0\), examine the objective function along the constraint curve.
  5. Calculate the quantities asked for and write the answer in the words of the problem.
!Common mistakes
  • The dimensions of the rectangle are confused with the coordinates of its corner. If the corner is \((x;\,y)\), the sides of the rectangle are \(2x\) and \(2y\), and its area is \(4xy\), not \(xy\).
  • The distance with the root is minimized. The calculations are much simpler when the squared distance is minimized – the smallest value is reached at the same point.
  • A needed solution is rejected. The coordinates of a point on a map may be negative, so a negative solution is rejected only when the variable is a quantity or a dimension.
  • The sufficient condition is checked at one point only. When there are several critical points, the value of \(\lambda\) obtained for each of them is substituted at that point.
  • A conclusion is drawn when \(D = 0\). In this case the test is not sufficient, so the function has to be examined along the constraint curve.
  • Gaussian elimination is applied to a non-linear system. Gaussian elimination is suitable only for systems of linear equations.

Example 1

Wheat and barley on a farm

Problem A farm grows wheat and barley. With its land, machines and workers it can produce \(x\) tonnes of wheat and \(y\) tonnes of barley a year. When all the resources are used fully, the possible combinations satisfy the equation \[ x^2 + 2y^2 = 6800 \] This curve is called the production possibilities curve. To produce more wheat, the farm has to produce less barley. Wheat is sold at 60 euros and barley at 80 euros per tonne.
How much wheat and barley should the farm produce to maximize the revenue? What is the maximum revenue?

1The model

The variables: \(x\) is the amount of wheat produced a year, in tonnes, and \(y\) is the amount of barley, in tonnes.

The objective function. The maximum revenue has to be found. A tonne of wheat is sold at 60 euros, so \(x\) tonnes bring \(60x\) euros. A tonne of barley is sold at 80 euros, so \(y\) tonnes bring \(80y\) euros. The revenue is the sum of these amounts:

\[ R(x;\,y) = 60x + 80y \]

The constraint. When all the resources are used fully, the production combination \((x;\,y)\) is on the production possibilities curve, so it satisfies its equation \(x^2 + 2y^2 = 6800\).

We look for the maximum revenue with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) when the production combination is on the production possibilities curve:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; R(x;\,y) = 60x + 80y \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x^2 + 2y^2 = 6800 \end{aligned} \]

The figure shows the lines of equal revenue \(60x + 80y = R\). The further a line is from the origin, the larger the revenue. The largest revenue is reached on the line that still has a common point with the curve, i.e. touches it.

2The Lagrangian function

We rewrite the constraint in the form \(x^2 + 2y^2 - 6800 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = 60x + 80y - \lambda\big(x^2 + 2y^2 - 6800\big) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= 60x + 80y \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda\big(x^2 + 2y^2 - 6800\big) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

The derivatives of the revenue function are constant: \((60x)'_x = 60\), \((80y)'_y = 80\). The derivative of the term \(-\lambda\big(x^2 + 2y^2 - 6800\big)\) with respect to \(x\) is \(-2\lambda x\), and with respect to \(y\) – \(-4\lambda y\):

\[ L'_x = 60 - 2\lambda x;\qquad L'_y = 80 - 4\lambda y \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 60 - 2\lambda x \\ L'_y = 80 - 4\lambda y \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 60 - 2\lambda x &= 0 \\ 80 - 4\lambda y &= 0 \\ x^2 + 2y^2 &= 6800 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the unknowns are multiplied by each other (\(\lambda x\), \(\lambda y\)), and in the constraint equation the variables are squared. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

4Solving the system

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first equation we move the term \(-2\lambda x\) to the right-hand side and divide both sides by \(2x\). In the second equation we move the term \(-4\lambda y\) and divide both sides by \(4y\). The quantities are positive, so \(x \ne 0\) and \(y \ne 0\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{60}{2x} = \frac{30}{x};\qquad \lambda = \frac{80}{4y} = \frac{20}{y} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = \frac{60}{2x} = \frac{30}{x} \\ \lambda = \frac{80}{4y} = \frac{20}{y} \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the two expressions equal and cross-multiply. Then we divide both sides by 30:

\[ \frac{30}{x} = \frac{20}{y} \;\Longrightarrow\; 30y = 20x \;\Longrightarrow\; y = \frac{2x}{3} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{30}{x} = \frac{20}{y} \\ \Longrightarrow\; 30y = 20x \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = \frac{2x}{3} \end{gathered} \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression \(y = \frac{2x}{3}\) into the constraint equation. Since \(y\) is squared, \(y^2 = \frac{4x^2}{9}\). We multiply both sides by 9 and divide them by 17:

\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 + 2\cdot\frac{4x^2}{9} = 6800 \;\Longrightarrow\; 9x^2 + 8x^2 = 61\,200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 17x^2 = 61\,200 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 = 3600 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 + 2\cdot\frac{4x^2}{9} = 6800 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 9x^2 + 8x^2 = 61\,200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 17x^2 = 61\,200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x^2 = 3600 \end{gathered} \]

We have obtained an incomplete quadratic equation. We move the number 3600 to the left-hand side and factor by the difference of squares formula (\(3600 = 60^2\)):

\[ x^2 - 3600 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 60)(x + 60) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 60\ \text{or}\ x = -60 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 - 3600 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; (x - 60)(x + 60) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 60\ \text{or}\ x = -60 \end{gathered} \]

The amount of wheat cannot be negative, so \(x = 60\). Then \(y = \frac{2\cdot 60}{3} = 40\), and \(\lambda = \frac{30}{60} = 0.5\). We obtain the critical point \(K(60;\,40)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 0.5\).

5The sufficient condition

The second order derivatives \(L''_{xx} = -2\lambda\), \(L''_{xy} = 0\) and \(L''_{yy} = -4\lambda\) depend on \(\lambda\), so they are not numbers. We substitute into them the value \(\lambda = 0.5\) obtained for the critical point \(K(60;\,40)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -2\cdot 0.5 = -1 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -4\cdot 0.5 = -2 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-1)\cdot(-2) - 0^2 = 2 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -2\cdot 0.5 = -1 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -4\cdot 0.5 = -2 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-1)\cdot(-2) - 0^2 = 2 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K(60;\,40)\) is a maximum point.

6The maximum revenue

We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the revenue function:

\[ R_{\max}(60;\,40) = 60\cdot 60 + 80\cdot 40 = 3600 + 3200 = 6800 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} R_{\max}(60;\,40) &= 60\cdot 60 + 80\cdot 40 \\ &= 3600 + 3200 = 6800 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The farm should produce 60 tonnes of wheat and 40 tonnes of barley a year. The maximum revenue is 6800 euros a year.

Example 2

A depot on a highway

Problem A distribution company serves three shops located at the points \(A(2;\,8)\), \(B(10;\,6)\) and \(C(6;\,4)\) of a map. The coordinates are in kilometres. The company wants to build a depot \(D(x;\,y)\) right next to a highway whose equation on the map is \(x + y = 8\). The depot should be placed so that the sum of the squared distances from it to the three shops is as small as possible.
Where should the depot be built?

1The variables and the squared distances

The location of the depot is chosen, so the variables are the coordinates \(x\) and \(y\) of the point \(D\).

The squared distance between the points \((x;\,y)\) and \((a;\,b)\) is \((x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2\) (see “Building the objective function”). Substituting the coordinates of each shop, we get the squared distances from \(D\):

\[ \begin{aligned} DA^2 &= (x - 2)^2 + (y - 8)^2 \\ DB^2 &= (x - 10)^2 + (y - 6)^2 \\ DC^2 &= (x - 6)^2 + (y - 4)^2 \end{aligned} \]

2The model

The objective function is the sum of these three squares:

\[ T(x;\,y) = (x - 2)^2 + (y - 8)^2 + (x - 10)^2 + (y - 6)^2 + (x - 6)^2 + (y - 4)^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &T(x;\,y) \\ &= (x - 2)^2 + (y - 8)^2 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ (x - 10)^2 + (y - 6)^2 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ (x - 6)^2 + (y - 4)^2 \end{aligned} \]

The constraint. The depot has to be right next to the highway, i.e. the point \(D\) is on the highway line. Therefore its coordinates satisfy the equation of the highway \(x + y = 8\).

We look for the minimum sum of the squared distances with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; T(x;\,y) \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 8 \end{aligned} \]

3The Lagrangian function

We rewrite the constraint in the form \(x + y - 8 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = T(x;\,y) - \lambda(x + y - 8) \]

4The system of equations

By the chain rule, \(\big((x - 2)^2\big)'_x = 2(x - 2)\). When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the terms that depend only on \(y\) are constants. After expanding the brackets and collecting like terms, we get:

\[ \begin{aligned} L'_x &= 2(x - 2) + 2(x - 10) + 2(x - 6) - \lambda = 6x - 36 - \lambda \\ L'_y &= 2(y - 8) + 2(y - 6) + 2(y - 4) - \lambda = 6y - 36 - \lambda \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} L'_x &= 2(x - 2) + 2(x - 10) \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 2(x - 6) - \lambda \\ &= 6x - 36 - \lambda \\[4pt] L'_y &= 2(y - 8) + 2(y - 6) \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 2(y - 4) - \lambda \\ &= 6y - 36 - \lambda \end{aligned} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 6x - 36 - \lambda &= 0 \\ 6y - 36 - \lambda &= 0 \\ x + y &= 8 \end{aligned} \right. \]

All the unknowns are of the first degree and are not multiplied by each other, so the system is linear.

5Solving the system by substitution

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first two equations we move the term \(-\lambda\) to the right-hand side:

\[ \lambda = 6x - 36;\qquad \lambda = 6y - 36 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = 6x - 36 \\ \lambda = 6y - 36 \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the two expressions equal. The number \(-36\) cancels on both sides, and then we divide both sides by 6:

\[ 6x - 36 = 6y - 36 \;\Longrightarrow\; 6x = 6y \;\Longrightarrow\; x = y \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 6x - 36 = 6y - 36 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 6x = 6y \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = y \end{gathered} \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression \(y = x\) into the constraint equation \(x + y = 8\):

\[ x + x = 8 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x = 8 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 4 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x + x = 8 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 2x = 8 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 4 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(y = x = 4\), and \(\lambda = 6\cdot 4 - 36 = -12\) (both expressions give the same value). We obtain the critical point \(K(4;\,4)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = -12\).

This system is linear, so it can also be solved by Gaussian elimination. If you prefer this method, the solution is given below.

Solution by Gaussian elimination

First we rearrange the equations: the unknowns are written on the left-hand side in the order \(x\), \(y\), \(\lambda\), and the numbers are moved to the right-hand side:

\[ \begin{aligned} 6x - 36 - \lambda = 0 \;&\Longrightarrow\; 6x - \lambda = 36 \\ 6y - 36 - \lambda = 0 \;&\Longrightarrow\; 6y - \lambda = 36 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 6x - 36 - \lambda = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 6x - \lambda = 36 \\[4pt] 6y - 36 - \lambda = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 6y - \lambda = 36 \end{gathered} \]

The second equation does not contain \(y\), and the third does not contain \(x\), so the corresponding elements of the matrix are 0.

The columns of the augmented matrix are the coefficients of the unknowns \(x\), \(y\) and \(\lambda\), and the last column contains the constant terms (see Topic 3). In the constraint equation the coefficient of \(x\) is 1, so it is written as the first row:

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 0 & 8 \\ 6 & 0 & -1 & 36 \\ 0 & 6 & -1 & 36 \end{array}\right) \]

The first element of the third row is already 0, so we multiply the first row by \(-6\) and add it to the second row only:

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} \htmlClass{hd}{1} & 1 & 0 & 8 \\ 6 & 0 & -1 & 36 \\ 0 & 6 & -1 & 36 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \htmlClass{ha}{(-6)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \\ \phantom{0} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 8 \\ \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -6 & -1 & -12 \\ 0 & 6 & -1 & 36 \end{array}\right)\)

The pivot element \(-6\) of the second row is not divided, to avoid fractions: the element 6 below it becomes zero when the second row is simply added to the third row (multiplier 1). We divide the new third row by \(-2\):

\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 8 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{-6} & -1 & -12 \\ 0 & 6 & -1 & 36 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{c} \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{(1)} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{\downarrow} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 8 \\ 0 & -6 & -1 & -12 \\ 0 & \htmlClass{hc}{0} & -2 & 24 \end{array}\right)\begin{array}{l} \phantom{0} \\ \phantom{0} \\ \htmlClass{ha}{{:}\,(-2)} \end{array}\)\(\Longrightarrow\)\(\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 0 & 8 \\ 0 & -6 & -1 & -12 \\ 0 & 0 & \htmlClass{hd}{1} & -12 \end{array}\right)\)

Back substitution. The third row gives \(\lambda = -12\). The second row stands for the equation \(-6y - \lambda = -12\), and the first for \(x + y = 8\):

\[ -6y + 12 = -12 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 4;\qquad x + 4 = 8 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 4 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} -6y + 12 = -12 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 4 \\ x + 4 = 8 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 4 \end{gathered} \]

The same solution is obtained: \(x = 4\), \(y = 4\), \(\lambda = -12\).

6The sufficient condition

The second order partial derivatives of the Lagrangian function are constant and do not depend on \(\lambda\), so their values at the critical point \(K\) are the same numbers:

\[ \begin{gathered} A = L''_{xx}(K) = 6;\quad B = L''_{xy}(K) = 0;\quad C = L''_{yy}(K) = 6 \\ D = AC - B^2 = 6\cdot 6 - 0^2 = 36 > 0 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = 6 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = 6 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= 6\cdot 6 - 0^2 \\ &= 36 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A > 0\), so \(K(4;\,4)\) is a minimum point.

7The minimum sum of the squared distances

We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the objective function:

\[ \begin{aligned} T_{\min}(4;\,4) &= \big[(4 - 2)^2 + (4 - 8)^2\big] + \big[(4 - 10)^2 + (4 - 6)^2\big] \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ \big[(4 - 6)^2 + (4 - 4)^2\big] \\ &= (4 + 16) + (36 + 4) + (4 + 0) = 64 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &T_{\min}(4;\,4) \\ &= \big[(4 - 2)^2 + (4 - 8)^2\big] \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ \big[(4 - 10)^2 + (4 - 6)^2\big] \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ \big[(4 - 6)^2 + (4 - 4)^2\big] \\ &= (4 + 16) + (36 + 4) + (4 + 0) \\ &= 64 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The depot should be built at the point \((4;\,4)\) of the highway. The minimum sum of the squared distances is 64.

Example 3

A rectangular platform in an elliptical square

Problem An events company is preparing an open-air festival in a city square shaped like an ellipse. The origin of the map is at the centre of the square, and the edge of the square is the ellipse \[ x^2 + 4y^2 = 7200 \] whose coordinates are in metres. The organisers want to set up a rectangular platform for the audience. The sides of the rectangle must be parallel to the coordinate axes, and its corners must be on the ellipse.
What are the dimensions of the largest such rectangle? What is its area?

1The model

The variables. Let \((x;\,y)\) be the corner of the rectangle in the first quadrant. The variables are the coordinates of this corner, \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\).

The objective function – the area. The ellipse is symmetric with respect to both coordinate axes, so the rectangle is symmetric too: its other corners are \((-x;\,y)\), \((-x;\,-y)\) and \((x;\,-y)\). Therefore the sides of the rectangle are \(2x\) and \(2y\) long, and its area is

\[ S(x;\,y) = 2x\cdot 2y = 4xy \]

The constraint. The corner \((x;\,y)\) is on the ellipse, so its coordinates satisfy the equation of the ellipse \(x^2 + 4y^2 = 7200\).

We look for the largest area with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) when the corner is on the ellipse:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; S(x;\,y) = 4xy \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x^2 + 4y^2 = 7200 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We rewrite the constraint in the form \(x^2 + 4y^2 - 7200 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = 4xy - \lambda\big(x^2 + 4y^2 - 7200\big) \]

3The system of equations

The derivatives of the area function are \((4xy)'_x = 4y\) and \((4xy)'_y = 4x\). The derivative of the constraint term with respect to \(x\) is \(-2\lambda x\), and with respect to \(y\) – \(-8\lambda y\):

\[ L'_x = 4y - 2\lambda x;\qquad L'_y = 4x - 8\lambda y \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 4y - 2\lambda x \\ L'_y = 4x - 8\lambda y \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 4y - 2\lambda x &= 0 \\ 4x - 8\lambda y &= 0 \\ x^2 + 4y^2 &= 7200 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the unknowns are multiplied by each other (\(\lambda x\), \(\lambda y\)), and in the constraint equation the variables are squared. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

4Solving the system

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first equation we move the term \(-2\lambda x\) to the right-hand side and divide both sides by \(2x\). In the second equation we move the term \(-8\lambda y\) and divide both sides by \(8y\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{4y}{2x} = \frac{2y}{x};\qquad \lambda = \frac{4x}{8y} = \frac{x}{2y} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = \frac{4y}{2x} = \frac{2y}{x} \\ \lambda = \frac{4x}{8y} = \frac{x}{2y} \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the two expressions equal and cross-multiply. Then we move all the terms to one side and factor by the difference of squares formula:

\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{2y}{x} = \frac{x}{2y} \;\Longrightarrow\; 4y^2 = x^2 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x^2 - 4y^2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 2y)(x + 2y) = 0 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{2y}{x} = \frac{x}{2y} \\ \Longrightarrow\; 4y^2 = x^2 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x^2 - 4y^2 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; (x - 2y)(x + 2y) = 0 \end{gathered} \]

The dimensions are positive, so \(x + 2y > 0\). This leaves \(x - 2y = 0\), i.e. \(x = 2y\).

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression \(x = 2y\) into the constraint equation (\((2y)^2 = 4y^2\)) and divide both sides by 8:

\[ (2y)^2 + 4y^2 = 7200 \;\Longrightarrow\; 8y^2 = 7200 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^2 = 900 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} (2y)^2 + 4y^2 = 7200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 8y^2 = 7200 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y^2 = 900 \end{gathered} \]

We factor the incomplete quadratic equation (\(900 = 30^2\)):

\[ y^2 - 900 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 30)(y + 30) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 30\ \text{or}\ y = -30 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} y^2 - 900 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; (y - 30)(y + 30) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 30\ \text{or}\ y = -30 \end{gathered} \]

We take the positive solution \(y = 30\). Then \(x = 2\cdot 30 = 60\), and \(\lambda = \frac{2\cdot 30}{60} = 1\). We obtain the critical point \(K(60;\,30)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 1\).

5The sufficient condition

The second order derivatives are \(L''_{xx} = -2\lambda\), \(L''_{xy} = 4\) and \(L''_{yy} = -8\lambda\). We substitute into them the value \(\lambda = 1\) obtained for the critical point \(K(60;\,30)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -2\cdot 1 = -2 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 4 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -8\cdot 1 = -8 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-2)\cdot(-8) - 4^2 = 16 - 16 = 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -2\cdot 1 = -2 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 4 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -8\cdot 1 = -8 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-2)\cdot(-8) - 4^2 = 16 - 16 \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D = 0\), so this test is not sufficient (see “When the test is not sufficient”). We examine the area along the admissible curve. The corner \((x;\,y)\) can be at any point of the arc of the ellipse in the first quadrant – from the point \(\big(\sqrt{7200};\,0\big)\) to the point \(\big(0;\,\sqrt{1800}\big)\). The area function is continuous on the closed arc, so it reaches a largest value. At the endpoints of the arc one dimension is zero, so the area is zero. Inside the arc the only critical point is \(K(60;\,30)\), so the area is largest there.

6The largest area and the dimensions

The sides of the rectangle are \(2x = 2\cdot 60 = 120\) metres and \(2y = 2\cdot 30 = 60\) metres, and its area is

\[ S_{\max}(60;\,30) = 4\cdot 60\cdot 30 = 7200 \]
Answer

The rectangle should be \(2\cdot 60 = 120\) metres long and \(2\cdot 30 = 60\) metres wide. Its area would be 7200 square metres.

Example 4

Connecting a factory to a ring road

Problem A city is surrounded by a circular ring road. The origin of the map is at the city centre. The ring road is the circle \[ x^2 + y^2 = 25 \] whose coordinates are in kilometres. A factory outside the city is located at the point \(F(6;\,8)\). It wants to build a straight access road to the ring road, as short as possible.
At which point should the access road join the ring road? How long would it be? Find the point of the ring road for which the squared distance to the factory is the smallest.

1The model

The variables \(x\) and \(y\) are the coordinates of the point where the access road joins the ring road.

The objective function. The road is straight, so its length is equal to the distance from \(F(6;\,8)\) to the point \((x;\,y)\):

\[ d = \sqrt{(x - 6)^2 + (y - 8)^2} \]

Instead of minimizing the distance \(d\), we minimize its square \(d^2 = (x - 6)^2 + (y - 8)^2\). Since the square root is an increasing function, \(d\) and \(d^2\) take their smallest values at the same point, and the derivatives of the squared distance are easier to calculate.

The constraint. The road has to reach the ring road, so the point \((x;\,y)\) is on the circle and satisfies its equation \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; f(x;\,y) = (x - 6)^2 + (y - 8)^2 \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x^2 + y^2 = 25 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We rewrite the constraint in the form \(x^2 + y^2 - 25 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) = (x - 6)^2 + (y - 8)^2 - \lambda\big(x^2 + y^2 - 25\big) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= (x - 6)^2 + (y - 8)^2 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda\big(x^2 + y^2 - 25\big) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

By the chain rule, \(\big((x - 6)^2\big)'_x = 2(x - 6)\), and the derivative of the constraint term with respect to \(x\) is \(-2\lambda x\). The derivative with respect to \(y\) is found in the same way:

\[ L'_x = 2(x - 6) - 2\lambda x;\qquad L'_y = 2(y - 8) - 2\lambda y \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 2(x - 6) - 2\lambda x \\ L'_y = 2(y - 8) - 2\lambda y \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 2(x - 6) - 2\lambda x &= 0 \\ 2(y - 8) - 2\lambda y &= 0 \\ x^2 + y^2 &= 25 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the unknowns are multiplied by each other (\(\lambda x\), \(\lambda y\)), and in the constraint equation the variables are squared. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

4Solving the system

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). We divide the first equation by 2 and get \(x - 6 - \lambda x = 0\). If \(x = 0\), we would get \(-6 = 0\), so \(x \ne 0\). We move the term \(-\lambda x\) to the right-hand side and divide by \(x\). The second equation is rearranged in the same way (\(y \ne 0\)):

\[ \lambda = \frac{x - 6}{x};\qquad \lambda = \frac{y - 8}{y} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = \frac{x - 6}{x} \\ \lambda = \frac{y - 8}{y} \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the expressions equal, multiply both sides by \(xy\) and expand the brackets. The term \(xy\) cancels on both sides:

\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{x - 6}{x} = \frac{y - 8}{y} \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 6)y = (y - 8)x \;\Longrightarrow\; xy - 6y = xy - 8x \\ \Longrightarrow\; -6y = -8x \;\Longrightarrow\; y = \frac{4x}{3} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{x - 6}{x} = \frac{y - 8}{y} \\ \Longrightarrow\; (x - 6)y = (y - 8)x \\ \Longrightarrow\; xy - 6y = xy - 8x \\ \Longrightarrow\; -6y = -8x \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = \frac{4x}{3} \end{gathered} \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression \(y = \frac{4x}{3}\) into the constraint equation (\(y^2 = \frac{16x^2}{9}\)). We multiply both sides by 9 and divide them by 25:

\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 + \frac{16x^2}{9} = 25 \;\Longrightarrow\; 9x^2 + 16x^2 = 225 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 25x^2 = 225 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 = 9 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 + \frac{16x^2}{9} = 25 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 9x^2 + 16x^2 = 225 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 25x^2 = 225 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x^2 = 9 \end{gathered} \]

We factor the incomplete quadratic equation:

\[ x^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 3)(x + 3) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3\ \text{or}\ x = -3 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 - 9 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; (x - 3)(x + 3) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 3\ \text{or}\ x = -3 \end{gathered} \]

Coordinates on a map can also be negative, so both solutions are suitable. For each of them we find \(y = \frac{4x}{3}\) and \(\lambda = \frac{x - 6}{x}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} x = 3:\quad &y = 4;\quad \lambda = \frac{3 - 6}{3} = -1 \\ x = -3:\quad &y = -4;\quad \lambda = \frac{-3 - 6}{-3} = 3 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} x = 3:\quad &y = 4;\quad \lambda = \frac{3 - 6}{3} = -1 \\ x = -3:\quad &y = -4 \\ &\lambda = \frac{-3 - 6}{-3} = 3 \end{aligned} \]

There are two critical points. We label them \(K_1(3;\,4)\), where \(\lambda = -1\), and \(K_2(-3;\,-4)\), where \(\lambda = 3\).

5The sufficient condition

The second order derivatives contain \(\lambda\), so their values are calculated at each critical point separately:

\[ L''_{xx} = 2 - 2\lambda;\qquad L''_{xy} = 0;\qquad L''_{yy} = 2 - 2\lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L''_{xx} = 2 - 2\lambda \\ L''_{xy} = 0 \\ L''_{yy} = 2 - 2\lambda \end{gathered} \]

The point \(K_1(3;\,4)\). We substitute the value \(\lambda = -1\) obtained for this point:

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K_1) = 2 - 2\cdot(-1) = 4 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K_1) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K_1) = 2 - 2\cdot(-1) = 4 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 4\cdot 4 - 0^2 = 16 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K_1) = 2 - 2\cdot(-1) \\ &= 4 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K_1) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K_1) = 2 - 2\cdot(-1) \\ &= 4 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 4\cdot 4 - 0^2 \\ &= 16 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A > 0\), so \(K_1\) is a minimum point.

The point \(K_2(-3;\,-4)\). We substitute the value \(\lambda = 3\) obtained for this point:

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K_2) = 2 - 2\cdot 3 = -4 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K_2) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K_2) = 2 - 2\cdot 3 = -4 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-4)\cdot(-4) - 0^2 = 16 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K_2) = 2 - 2\cdot 3 = -4 \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K_2) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K_2) = 2 - 2\cdot 3 = -4 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-4)\cdot(-4) - 0^2 = 16 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K_2\) is a maximum point.

The point \(K_1(3;\,4)\) is the closest to the factory, and the point \(K_2(-3;\,-4)\) is the farthest, on the other side of the city.

6The length of the shortest road

The length of the road is equal to the distance from \(F(6;\,8)\) to the point \(K_1(3;\,4)\):

\[ d = \sqrt{(3 - 6)^2 + (4 - 8)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} d &= \sqrt{(3 - 6)^2 + (4 - 8)^2} \\ &= \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The access road should reach the ring road at the point \((3;\,4)\). It would be 5 km long.

Example 5

Working hours of a freelance designer

Problem A freelance designer can spend 16 hours a day on work and leisure. She earns 20 euros per hour of work and, in addition, receives 80 euros a day from renting out a flat. All her income is spent on consumption. Her daily utility from spending \(c\) euros and having \(l\) hours of leisure is \[ U(c;\,l) = c^{0.5}l^{0.5} \] How many hours a day should the designer work to maximize her utility? How much would she spend, and what would the maximum utility be?

1The variables and the constraint

The variables: \(c\) is the daily consumption spending in euros, and \(l\) is the number of hours of leisure a day. This time the variables are denoted not by \(x\) and \(y\) but by letters that remind us of their meaning.

The constraint is built step by step:

  • 16 hours are spent on work and leisure, so with \(l\) hours of leisure the designer works \(16 - l\) hours;
  • she earns 20 euros for each hour of work, so she earns \(20(16 - l)\) euros;
  • adding the rental income of 80 euros, we get the total daily income. All of it is spent on consumption, so it is equal to \(c\).
\[ \begin{gathered} c = 20(16 - l) + 80 \;\Longrightarrow\; c = 320 - 20l + 80 \\ \Longrightarrow\; c = 400 - 20l \;\Longrightarrow\; c + 20l = 400 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} c = 20(16 - l) + 80 \\ \Longrightarrow\; c = 320 - 20l + 80 \\ \Longrightarrow\; c = 400 - 20l \\ \Longrightarrow\; c + 20l = 400 \end{gathered} \]

The number 400 is the income the designer would get by working all 16 hours (\(20\cdot 16 + 80 = 400\)). Each hour of leisure costs her 20 euros of lost income.

2The model

The objective function – the utility \(U(c;\,l)\), given in the problem. We look for the maximum utility with respect to \(c\) and \(l\) subject to the constraint:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{c;\,l}\; U(c;\,l) = c^{0.5}l^{0.5} \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; c + 20l = 400 \end{aligned} \]

3The Lagrangian function

We rewrite the constraint in the form \(c + 20l - 400 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ L(c;\,l;\,\lambda) = c^{0.5}l^{0.5} - \lambda(c + 20l - 400) \]

4The system of equations

When differentiating with respect to \(c\), the factor \(l^{0.5}\) is constant: \(\big(c^{0.5}\big)'_c = 0.5c^{-0.5}\). When differentiating with respect to \(l\), the constant factor is \(c^{0.5}\). The derivatives of the constraint term are \(-\lambda\) and \(-20\lambda\):

\[ L'_c = 0.5c^{-0.5}l^{0.5} - \lambda;\qquad L'_l = 0.5c^{0.5}l^{-0.5} - 20\lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_c = 0.5c^{-0.5}l^{0.5} - \lambda \\ L'_l = 0.5c^{0.5}l^{-0.5} - 20\lambda \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 0.5c^{-0.5}l^{0.5} - \lambda &= 0 \\ 0.5c^{0.5}l^{-0.5} - 20\lambda &= 0 \\ c + 20l &= 400 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables are raised to fractional powers and multiplied by each other. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

5Solving the system by dividing the equations

In the first two equations we move the terms with \(\lambda\) to the right-hand sides:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 0.5c^{-0.5}l^{0.5} &= \lambda \\ 0.5c^{0.5}l^{-0.5} &= 20\lambda \end{aligned} \right. \]

Dividing the equations. We divide the first equation by the second. The factors \(0.5\) and \(\lambda\) cancel, and the exponents are subtracted: \(c^{-0.5 - 0.5} = c^{-1}\), \(l^{0.5 - (-0.5)} = l\):

\[ \frac{0.5c^{-0.5}l^{0.5}}{0.5c^{0.5}l^{-0.5}} = \frac{\lambda}{20\lambda} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{l}{c} = \frac{1}{20} \;\Longrightarrow\; c = 20l \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{0.5c^{-0.5}l^{0.5}}{0.5c^{0.5}l^{-0.5}} = \frac{\lambda}{20\lambda} \\ \Longrightarrow\; \frac{l}{c} = \frac{1}{20} \\ \Longrightarrow\; c = 20l \end{gathered} \]

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression \(c = 20l\) into the constraint equation \(c + 20l = 400\):

\[ 20l + 20l = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; 40l = 400 \;\Longrightarrow\; l = 10 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 20l + 20l = 400 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 40l = 400 \\ \Longrightarrow\; l = 10 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(c = 20\cdot 10 = 200\). We obtain the critical point \(K(200;\,10)\). The designer works \(16 - 10 = 6\) hours.

6The type of the critical point

The utility function is a Cobb-Douglas function with \(A = 1 > 0\) and \(\alpha + \beta = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 \le 1\), so it is concave. The constraint is linear, so \(L\) is also concave (see “Cobb-Douglas functions in constrained optimization problems”), and \(K(200;\,10)\) is a maximum point.

7The maximum utility

We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the utility function. Since \(a^{0.5}b^{0.5} = \sqrt{ab}\):

\[ U_{\max}(200;\,10) = \sqrt{200\cdot 10} = \sqrt{2000} \approx 44.72 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} U_{\max}(200;\,10) &= \sqrt{200\cdot 10} \\ &= \sqrt{2000} \approx 44.72 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The designer should work 6 hours a day, spend 10 hours on leisure and spend 200 euros a day on consumption. The maximum utility is approximately 44.72.

Example 6

Advertising in two cities

Problem A retail chain has 60 thousand euros for advertising in two cities. If it spends \(x\) thousand euros in city A and \(y\) thousand euros in city B, the additional gross profit from sales, in thousands of euros, is
\[ G(x;\,y) = 300\big(1 - e^{-0.1x}\big) + 600\big(1 - e^{-0.05y}\big) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} G(x;\,y) &= 300\big(1 - e^{-0.1x}\big) \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 600\big(1 - e^{-0.05y}\big) \end{aligned} \]
In each city the additional profit grows quickly at first, and then its growth slows down. The first euros spent on advertising reach many new customers, and when the spending is increased further, fewer additional customers are reached.
a) How should the budget be divided between the two cities to maximize the additional gross profit? What is the maximum additional gross profit?
b) How would this maximum change if the budget increased by one thousand euros?

1The model

The variables: \(x\) and \(y\) are the advertising spending in cities A and B, in thousands of euros.

The objective function – the additional gross profit \(G(x;\,y)\), given in the problem. Each of its terms, for example \(300\big(1 - e^{-0.1x}\big)\), increases as the spending increases, but does not exceed 300, because \(e^{-0.1x}\) approaches zero. In this way the function reflects the slowing growth of profit described in the problem.

The constraint. The whole budget is spent, so the spending in the two cities adds up to 60 thousand euros: \(x + y = 60\).

We look for the maximum additional gross profit with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) when the whole budget is spent:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; G(x;\,y) = 300\big(1 - e^{-0.1x}\big) + 600\big(1 - e^{-0.05y}\big) \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 60 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; G(x;\,y) \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x + y = 60 \end{aligned} \]

2The Lagrangian function

We rewrite the constraint in the form \(x + y - 60 = 0\) and build the Lagrangian function:

\[ \begin{aligned} L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) &= 300\big(1 - e^{-0.1x}\big) + 600\big(1 - e^{-0.05y}\big) \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(x + y - 60) \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &L(x;\,y;\,\lambda) \\ &= 300\big(1 - e^{-0.1x}\big) \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 600\big(1 - e^{-0.05y}\big) \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- \lambda(x + y - 60) \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

By the chain rule, \(\big(e^{-0.1x}\big)'_x = -0.1e^{-0.1x}\), so

\[ \Big(300\big(1 - e^{-0.1x}\big)\Big)'_x = 300\cdot 0.1e^{-0.1x} = 30e^{-0.1x} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\Big(300\big(1 - e^{-0.1x}\big)\Big)'_x \\ &= 300\cdot 0.1e^{-0.1x} = 30e^{-0.1x} \end{aligned} \]

In the same way, \(\big(600(1 - e^{-0.05y})\big)'_y = 600\cdot 0.05e^{-0.05y} = 30e^{-0.05y}\). The derivative of the constraint term with respect to each variable is \(-\lambda\):

\[ L'_x = 30e^{-0.1x} - \lambda;\qquad L'_y = 30e^{-0.05y} - \lambda \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L'_x = 30e^{-0.1x} - \lambda \\ L'_y = 30e^{-0.05y} - \lambda \end{gathered} \]

We set both derivatives equal to zero and add the constraint equation to the system:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 30e^{-0.1x} - \lambda &= 0 \\ 30e^{-0.05y} - \lambda &= 0 \\ x + y &= 60 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables \(x\) and \(y\) are in the exponents of exponential functions. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

4Solving the system

The expressions for the multiplier \(\lambda\). In the first two equations we move the term \(-\lambda\) to the right-hand side:

\[ \lambda = 30e^{-0.1x};\qquad \lambda = 30e^{-0.05y} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \lambda = 30e^{-0.1x} \\ \lambda = 30e^{-0.05y} \end{gathered} \]

An equation in \(x\) and \(y\). We set the two expressions equal and divide both sides by 30. Then we take the logarithm of both sides of the equation and use the rules \(\ln\big(a^n\big) = n\ln a\) and \(\ln e = 1\):

\[ 30e^{-0.1x} = 30e^{-0.05y} \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{-0.1x} = e^{-0.05y} \;\Longrightarrow\; \ln e^{-0.1x} = \ln e^{-0.05y} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 30e^{-0.1x} = 30e^{-0.05y} \\ \Longrightarrow\; e^{-0.1x} = e^{-0.05y} \\ \Longrightarrow\; \ln e^{-0.1x} = \ln e^{-0.05y} \end{gathered} \]
\[ -0.1x\cdot\ln e = -0.05y\cdot\ln e \;\Longrightarrow\; -0.1x = -0.05y \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 2x \]
\[ \begin{gathered} -0.1x\cdot\ln e = -0.05y\cdot\ln e \\ \Longrightarrow\; -0.1x = -0.05y \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 2x \end{gathered} \]

In the last step both sides were divided by \(-0.05\).

Substituting into the constraint. We substitute the expression \(y = 2x\) into the constraint equation \(x + y = 60\):

\[ x + 2x = 60 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3x = 60 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 20 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x + 2x = 60 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 3x = 60 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 20 \end{gathered} \]

Then \(y = 2\cdot 20 = 40\), and \(\lambda = 30e^{-0.1\cdot 20} = 30e^{-2} \approx 4.06\). We obtain the critical point \(K(20;\,40)\) and the value of the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda = 30e^{-2}\).

5The sufficient condition

The second order partial derivatives are

\[ L''_{xx} = -3e^{-0.1x};\qquad L''_{xy} = 0;\qquad L''_{yy} = -1.5e^{-0.05y} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} L''_{xx} = -3e^{-0.1x} \\ L''_{xy} = 0 \\ L''_{yy} = -1.5e^{-0.05y} \end{gathered} \]

The derivatives depend on \(x\) and \(y\), so we substitute the coordinates of the critical point \(K(20;\,40)\). At this point \(e^{-0.1x} = e^{-0.1\cdot 20} = e^{-2}\) and \(e^{-0.05y} = e^{-0.05\cdot 40} = e^{-2}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -3e^{-0.1\cdot 20} = -3e^{-2} \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -1.5e^{-0.05\cdot 40} = -1.5e^{-2} \\ D &= AC - B^2 = \big(-3e^{-2}\big)\cdot\big(-1.5e^{-2}\big) - 0^2 = 4.5e^{-4} > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -3e^{-0.1\cdot 20} \\ &= -3e^{-2} \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -1.5e^{-0.05\cdot 40} \\ &= -1.5e^{-2} \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= \big(-3e^{-2}\big)\cdot\big(-1.5e^{-2}\big) - 0^2 \\ &= 4.5e^{-4} > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K(20;\,40)\) is a maximum point.

6The maximum profit and its change

a) At the critical point \(e^{-0.1\cdot 20} = e^{-0.05\cdot 40} = e^{-2}\):

\[ G_{\max}(20;\,40) = 300\big(1 - e^{-2}\big) + 600\big(1 - e^{-2}\big) = 900\big(1 - e^{-2}\big) \approx 778.20 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &G_{\max}(20;\,40) \\ &= 300\big(1 - e^{-2}\big) + 600\big(1 - e^{-2}\big) \\ &= 900\big(1 - e^{-2}\big) \approx 778.20 \end{aligned} \]

b) The budget increases by one thousand euros, i.e. \(\Delta m = 1\). The Lagrange multiplier is \(\lambda = 30e^{-2} \approx 4.06\) (see the meaning of the Lagrange multiplier):

\[ \Delta G^{*} \approx \lambda\cdot\Delta m = 4.06\cdot 1 = 4.06 \]
Answer

a) The chain should spend 20 thousand euros on advertising in city A and 40 thousand euros in city B. The maximum additional gross profit is approximately 778.20 thousand euros. b) It would increase by approximately 4.06 thousand euros.

A hotel has an elliptical courtyard. The origin of the map is at the centre of the courtyard, and the edge of the courtyard is given by the equation \(4x^2 + 9y^2 = 7200\). The coordinates are in metres. A rectangular terrace is to be built in the courtyard, with its sides parallel to the axes and its corners on the edge of the courtyard.

What are the dimensions of the largest such terrace? What is its area?

The model. One corner of the terrace is \((x;\,y)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{x;\,y}\; S(x;\,y) = 4xy \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; 4x^2 + 9y^2 = 7200 \end{aligned} \]

The system of equations and the critical point (the system is non-linear; \(\lambda = \frac{y}{2x} = \frac{2x}{9y}\), so \(9y^2 = 4x^2\) and \(2x = 3y\)):

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 4y - 8\lambda x &= 0 \\ 4x - 18\lambda y &= 0 \\ 4x^2 + 9y^2 &= 7200 \end{aligned} \right. \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x = 3y \;\Longrightarrow\; K(30;\,20),\ \lambda = \frac{1}{3} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \left\{ \begin{aligned} 4y - 8\lambda x &= 0 \\ 4x - 18\lambda y &= 0 \\ 4x^2 + 9y^2 &= 7200 \end{aligned} \right. \\ \Longrightarrow\; 2x = 3y \\ \Longrightarrow\; K(30;\,20),\ \lambda = \frac{1}{3} \end{gathered} \]

Substituting \(x = 1.5y\) into the constraint gives \(18y^2 = 7200\). We factor the incomplete quadratic equation:

\[ y^2 - 400 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 20)(y + 20) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 20,\quad x = 30 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} y^2 - 400 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; (y - 20)(y + 20) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 20,\quad x = 30 \end{gathered} \]

The sufficient condition:

The second order derivatives: \(L''_{xx} = -8\lambda\), \(L''_{xy} = 4\), \(L''_{yy} = -18\lambda\). We substitute \(\lambda = \frac{1}{3}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -8\cdot\frac{1}{3} = -\frac{8}{3} \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 4 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -18\cdot\frac{1}{3} = -6 \\ D &= \Big(-\frac{8}{3}\Big)\cdot(-6) - 4^2 = 16 - 16 = 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= L''_{xx}(K) = -8\cdot\frac{1}{3} = -\frac{8}{3} \\ B &= L''_{xy}(K) = 4 \\ C &= L''_{yy}(K) = -18\cdot\frac{1}{3} = -6 \\ D &= \Big(-\frac{8}{3}\Big)\cdot(-6) - 4^2 \\ &= 16 - 16 = 0 \end{aligned} \]

This test is not sufficient. The area function is continuous on the closed arc of the ellipse in the first quadrant. At the endpoints of the arc the area is zero, and the only interior critical point is \(K(30;\,20)\), so the area is largest there.

A circular metro line on a map has the equation \(x^2 + y^2 = 100\). The origin is at the city centre, and the coordinates are in kilometres. A business park is located at the point \(F(12;\,9)\). A bicycle path, as short as possible, is to be built from the park to the metro line.

At which point should the path reach the metro line, and how long would it be? Minimize the squared distance from \(F\) to a point of the metro line.

The model:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\min_{x;\,y}\; f(x;\,y) = (x - 12)^2 + (y - 9)^2 \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; x^2 + y^2 = 100 \end{aligned} \]

The system of equations and the critical points (the system is non-linear; \(\lambda = \frac{x - 12}{x} = \frac{y - 9}{y}\), so \(y = 0.75x\)):

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 2(x - 12) - 2\lambda x &= 0 \\ 2(y - 9) - 2\lambda y &= 0 \\ x^2 + y^2 &= 100 \end{aligned} \right. \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 0.75x \;\Longrightarrow\; K_1(8;\,6),\ K_2(-8;\,-6) \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \left\{ \begin{aligned} 2(x - 12) - 2\lambda x &= 0 \\ 2(y - 9) - 2\lambda y &= 0 \\ x^2 + y^2 &= 100 \end{aligned} \right. \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 0.75x \\ \Longrightarrow\; K_1(8;\,6),\ K_2(-8;\,-6) \end{gathered} \]

Substituting \(y = 0.75x\) into the constraint gives \(1.5625x^2 = 100\). We factor the incomplete quadratic equation:

\[ x^2 - 64 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 8)(x + 8) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 8\ \text{or}\ x = -8 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 - 64 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; (x - 8)(x + 8) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 8\ \text{or}\ x = -8 \end{gathered} \]

The second order derivatives: \(L''_{xx} = 2 - 2\lambda\), \(L''_{xy} = 0\), \(L''_{yy} = 2 - 2\lambda\).

How many hours a day should the student work to maximize the utility? How much would the student spend?

The constraint:

\[ c = 15(10 - l) + 30 \;\Longrightarrow\; c + 15l = 180 \]

\[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{c;\,l}\; U(c;\,l) = c^{0.5}l^{0.5} \\ &\text{subject to}\;\; c + 15l = 180 \end{aligned} \]
