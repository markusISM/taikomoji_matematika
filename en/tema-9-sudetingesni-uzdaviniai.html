Advanced Optimization Problems

In the previous section the objective function was given, or it was built by a familiar formula such as \(P = R - TC\). In the problems of this section the most important step is to build the objective function from the description of the situation. Once it is built, it is optimized in the same way, with the necessary and the sufficient conditions of an extremum.

Building the objective function

Build the objective function by answering three questions.

  1. Which quantities can be chosen? These two quantities are the variables. They may be the coordinates of a point on a map, amounts of money, the points where a cable route changes direction, the dimensions of a building, working conditions, a temperature and an amount of fertiliser.
  2. Which quantity has to be maximized or minimized, and of which parts does it consist? Write each part through the variables and add the parts. They may be the squared distances to several shops, the costs of the separate parts of a cable route, or the costs related to the walls, the roof and the floor.
  3. Does a condition of the problem fix the value of a third quantity? For example, if the volume of a building is given, express its height through its length and width and substitute it into the objective function. The function then depends on two variables only.

Several formulas are often needed in this section: the squared distance between the points \((x;\,y)\) and \((a;\,b)\) is \((x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2\) (the Pythagorean theorem), the hypotenuse of a right triangle with the legs \(a\) and \(x\) is \(\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}\), and the volume of a rectangular box is \(xyz\).

Methods of solving the system of equations

In the problems of this section the objective function is often not a polynomial of the second degree, so the system of the necessary condition is usually non-linear. Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it (see “The system of the necessary condition”). The examples use the following methods:

  • One variable in each equation. The equations are solved separately (Examples 1, 3 and 4).
  • Substitution. If one of the equations is linear, one variable is expressed from it and substituted into the other equation (Example 2). If both equations contain the same complicated expression, it is convenient to replace it by a new variable (Example 7).
  • Dividing the equations. If both equations contain the same variables in different powers, dividing one equation by the other makes the powers cancel (Example 5).
  • A product equal to zero. A product is equal to zero when at least one of its factors is equal to zero, so cases are considered. An equation must not be divided by a variable that may be equal to zero – solutions are lost in this way (Example 6).
  • Quadratic equations. An incomplete quadratic equation, for example \(x^2 - 9 = 0\), is conveniently solved by factoring: \((x - 3)(x + 3) = 0\). A complete quadratic equation is solved with the discriminant.
  • Roots. When an equation with a root is squared, extraneous solutions may appear, so the solutions obtained must be checked in the original equation (Example 4).

Finally, reject the solutions that do not satisfy the conditions of the problem, for example negative spending or negative dimensions. If the denominator of a fraction must not be equal to zero, state which values the variable cannot take, and check that they are different from the solutions found.

How to solve

  1. Answer the three questions and build the objective function (see “Building the objective function”). If the function depends on three variables, express one of them through the others by the condition of the problem.
  2. Find the first order partial derivatives, set them equal to zero and solve the system of equations (see “Methods of solving the system of equations”).
  3. Use the sufficient condition to decide the type of each critical point. Label the critical point with the letter \(K\), and if there are several, with \(K_1\), \(K_2\), and so on. Obtain the values of the second derivatives \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) by substituting the coordinates of the critical point into the derivatives. If there are several critical points, check the sufficient condition at each of them separately. The critical points may include relative maxima, relative minima and saddle points.
  4. Reject the points that do not satisfy the conditions of the problem, for example when the spending would be negative.
  5. Calculate the quantities asked for and write the answer in the words of the problem.
!Common mistakes
  • A condition is ignored when the objective function is built. For example, the weight is left out (goods delivered twice), or one of the parts of the cost is forgotten.
  • The third variable is not eliminated. If the function depends on three variables, one of them must be expressed through the others by the condition of the problem (for example, by the volume).
  • Gaussian elimination is applied to a non-linear system. Gaussian elimination is suitable only for systems of linear equations.
  • An equation is divided by a variable. If the variable may be equal to zero, solutions are lost in this way. Set the product equal to zero and consider the cases.
  • The solutions are not checked after squaring. Squaring can produce extraneous solutions.
  • The sufficient condition is checked at one point only. When there are several critical points, the type must be decided at each of them.

Example 1

Location of a distribution centre

Problem A retail chain has three shops located at the points \(A(1;\,2)\), \(B(9;\,4)\) and \(C(5;\,10)\) of a city map. The coordinates are in kilometres. Shop A receives deliveries twice a day, and shops B and C once a day each. The chain wants to build a distribution centre at a point \(W(x;\,y)\) where the sum of the squared distances from the centre to the shops is as small as possible. The squared distance to each shop is counted in the sum as many times as goods are delivered to it per day.
Where should the distribution centre be built?

1The variables and the squared distances

The location of the distribution centre is chosen, so the variables are the coordinates \(x\) and \(y\) of the point \(W\).

The distance between the points \((x;\,y)\) and \((a;\,b)\) is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. Its legs are the horizontal difference \(x - a\) and the vertical difference \(y - b\), so by the Pythagorean theorem the squared distance is

\[ (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 \]

Substituting the coordinates of each shop, we get the squared distances from \(W\):

\[ \begin{aligned} WA^2 &= (x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2 \\ WB^2 &= (x - 9)^2 + (y - 4)^2 \\ WC^2 &= (x - 5)^2 + (y - 10)^2 \end{aligned} \]

2The objective function

Goods are delivered to shop A twice a day, so its squared distance is written in the sum twice, i.e. it is multiplied by 2. The squared distances to B and C are written once each. Adding them gives the objective function whose smallest value we are looking for:

\[ T(x;\,y) = 2\big[(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2\big] + \big[(x - 9)^2 + (y - 4)^2\big] + \big[(x - 5)^2 + (y - 10)^2\big] \]
\[ \begin{aligned} T(x;\,y) &= 2\big[(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2\big] \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ \big[(x - 9)^2 + (y - 4)^2\big] \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ \big[(x - 5)^2 + (y - 10)^2\big] \end{aligned} \]

3The first order partial derivatives

Each squared expression is a composite function, so \(\big((x - 1)^2\big)'_x = 2(x - 1)\). When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the terms that depend only on \(y\) are constants, so their derivatives are 0. After expanding the brackets and collecting like terms, we get

\[ \begin{aligned} T'_x &= 2\cdot 2(x - 1) + 2(x - 9) + 2(x - 5) = 4x - 4 + 2x - 18 + 2x - 10 = 8x - 32 \\ T'_y &= 2\cdot 2(y - 2) + 2(y - 4) + 2(y - 10) = 4y - 8 + 2y - 8 + 2y - 20 = 8y - 36 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} T'_x &= 2\cdot 2(x - 1) + 2(x - 9) + 2(x - 5) \\ &= 4x - 4 + 2x - 18 + 2x - 10 \\ &= 8x - 32 \\[4pt] T'_y &= 2\cdot 2(y - 2) + 2(y - 4) + 2(y - 10) \\ &= 4y - 8 + 2y - 8 + 2y - 20 \\ &= 8y - 36 \end{aligned} \]

4The system of equations and its solution

We set both partial derivatives equal to zero:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 8x - 32 &= 0 \\ 8y - 36 &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \]

The system is linear, but each equation contains only one variable. Therefore neither substitution nor Gaussian elimination is needed – the equations are solved separately. We move the number to the right-hand side and divide both sides by 8:

\[ \begin{aligned} 8x &= 32 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 4 \\ 8y &= 36 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 4.5 \end{aligned} \]

We obtain the critical point \(K(4;\,4.5)\).

5The sufficient condition

The second order partial derivatives \(T''_{xx} = 8\), \(T''_{xy} = 0\) and \(T''_{yy} = 8\) are constant, so their values at the critical point \(K\) are the same numbers:

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= T''_{xx}(K) = 8 \\ B &= T''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= T''_{yy}(K) = 8 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 8\cdot 8 - 0^2 = 64 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= T''_{xx}(K) = 8 \\ B &= T''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= T''_{yy}(K) = 8 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 8\cdot 8 - 0^2 \\ &= 64 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A > 0\), so \(K(4;\,4.5)\) is a minimum point.

6The minimum sum of the squared distances

We substitute the coordinates of the critical point into the objective function:

\[ \begin{aligned} T_{\min}(4;\,4.5) &= 2\big[(4 - 1)^2 + (4.5 - 2)^2\big] + \big[(4 - 9)^2 + (4.5 - 4)^2\big] \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ \big[(4 - 5)^2 + (4.5 - 10)^2\big] \\ &= 2(9 + 6.25) + (25 + 0.25) + (1 + 30.25) = 87 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &T_{\min}(4;\,4.5) \\ &= 2\big[(4 - 1)^2 + (4.5 - 2)^2\big] \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ \big[(4 - 9)^2 + (4.5 - 4)^2\big] \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ \big[(4 - 5)^2 + (4.5 - 10)^2\big] \\ &= 2(9 + 6.25) + (25 + 0.25) \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ (1 + 30.25) = 87 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The distribution centre should be built at the point \((4;\,4.5)\) of the map. The minimum sum of the squared distances is 87.

Example 2

Memberships and personal training in a fitness club

Problem A fitness club sells \(x\) hundred monthly memberships and \(y\) hundred personal training sessions a month. Members also buy training sessions, so the profit function contains the term \(12xy\). When the club becomes crowded, the costs grow quickly. The monthly profit, in thousands of euros, is
\[ P(x;\,y) = -x^3 + 12xy - 2y^2 - 21x - 20y - 10 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} P(x;\,y) &= -x^3 + 12xy - 2y^2 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- 21x - 20y - 10 \end{aligned} \]
Find all critical points of the profit function and classify them. How many memberships and training sessions should be sold to maximize the profit? What is the maximum profit?

1The first order partial derivatives

The profit function is given. When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the term \(12xy\) has the constant factor \(12y\), and the terms \(-2y^2\), \(-20y\) and \(-10\) are constants. With respect to \(y\) it is the other way round:

\[ P'_x = -3x^2 + 12y - 21;\qquad P'_y = 12x - 4y - 20 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} P'_x = -3x^2 + 12y - 21; \\ P'_y = 12x - 4y - 20 \end{gathered} \]

2The system of equations

We set both partial derivatives equal to zero:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} -3x^2 + 12y - 21 &= 0 \\ 12x - 4y - 20 &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the first equation contains \(x^2\). Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

However, the second equation is linear, so it is easy to express one variable from it and substitute it into the first equation – the system is solved by substitution.

3Solving the system by substitution

We express \(y\) from the second equation. We move the term \(-4y\) to the right-hand side (its sign changes) and divide both sides by 4:

\[ 12x - 4y - 20 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 4y = 12x - 20 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 3x - 5 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 12x - 4y - 20 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 4y = 12x - 20 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 3x - 5 \end{gathered} \]

We substitute the expression into the first equation in place of \(y\). Since \(y\) is multiplied by 12, the expression is written in brackets:

\[ -3x^2 + 12(3x - 5) - 21 = 0 \]

We expand the brackets and collect like terms:

\[ -3x^2 + 36x - 60 - 21 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; -3x^2 + 36x - 81 = 0 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} -3x^2 + 36x - 60 - 21 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; -3x^2 + 36x - 81 = 0 \end{gathered} \]

We divide both sides by \(-3\) and obtain a quadratic equation:

\[ x^2 - 12x + 27 = 0 \]

We solve it with the discriminant:

\[ D = (-12)^2 - 4\cdot 1\cdot 27 = 144 - 108 = 36;\qquad \sqrt{D} = 6 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} D = (-12)^2 - 4\cdot 1\cdot 27 \\ = 144 - 108 = 36 \\ \sqrt{D} = 6 \end{gathered} \]
\[ x_1 = \frac{12 - 6}{2} = 3;\qquad x_2 = \frac{12 + 6}{2} = 9 \]

We substitute each value of \(x\) into the expression \(y = 3x - 5\):

\[ y_1 = 3\cdot 3 - 5 = 4;\qquad y_2 = 3\cdot 9 - 5 = 22 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} y_1 = 3\cdot 3 - 5 = 4; \\ y_2 = 3\cdot 9 - 5 = 22 \end{gathered} \]

There are two critical points. We label them \(K_1(3;\,4)\) and \(K_2(9;\,22)\).

4The sufficient condition

The second order partial derivatives are

\[ P''_{xx} = -6x;\qquad P''_{xy} = 12;\qquad P''_{yy} = -4 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} P''_{xx} = -6x; \\ P''_{xy} = 12; \\ P''_{yy} = -4 \end{gathered} \]

The derivative \(P''_{xx}\) contains \(x\), so \(A\) depends on the point, and each critical point is tested separately.

The point \(K_1(3;\,4)\). We substitute \(x = 3\) and \(y = 4\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{xx}(K_1) = -6\cdot 3 = -18 \\ B &= P''_{xy}(K_1) = 12 \\ C &= P''_{yy}(K_1) = -4 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-18)\cdot(-4) - 12^2 = 72 - 144 = -72 < 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{xx}(K_1) = -6\cdot 3 = -18 \\ B &= P''_{xy}(K_1) = 12 \\ C &= P''_{yy}(K_1) = -4 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-18)\cdot(-4) - 12^2 \\ &= 72 - 144 = -72 < 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D < 0\), so \(K_1\) is a saddle point.

The point \(K_2(9;\,22)\). We substitute \(x = 9\) and \(y = 22\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{xx}(K_2) = -6\cdot 9 = -54 \\ B &= P''_{xy}(K_2) = 12 \\ C &= P''_{yy}(K_2) = -4 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-54)\cdot(-4) - 12^2 = 216 - 144 = 72 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{xx}(K_2) = -6\cdot 9 = -54 \\ B &= P''_{xy}(K_2) = 12 \\ C &= P''_{yy}(K_2) = -4 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-54)\cdot(-4) - 12^2 \\ &= 216 - 144 = 72 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K_2\) is a maximum point.

5The maximum profit

We substitute the coordinates of the point \(K_2(9;\,22)\) into the profit function:

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\max}(9;\,22) &= -9^3 + 12\cdot 9\cdot 22 - 2\cdot 22^2 - 21\cdot 9 - 20\cdot 22 - 10 \\ &= -729 + 2376 - 968 - 189 - 440 - 10 = 40 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &P_{\max}(9;\,22) \\ &= -9^3 + 12\cdot 9\cdot 22 - 2\cdot 22^2 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- 21\cdot 9 - 20\cdot 22 - 10 \\ &= -729 + 2376 - 968 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- 189 - 440 - 10 = 40 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The point \(K_1(3;\,4)\) is a saddle point. The maximum profit is 40 thousand euros a month, when 900 memberships and 2200 personal training sessions are sold a month.

Example 3

Development and promotion budget

Problem A company launches a new product. It will be sold at 50 euros, and the cost of producing one unit is 30 euros. If the company spends \(x\) thousand euros on its development and \(y\) thousand euros on its promotion, it expects to sell \[ N(x;\,y) = \frac{3200x}{x + 4} + \frac{5000y}{y + 9} \] units. How much should the company spend on development and on promotion to maximize the profit? How many units would it sell, and what would the maximum profit be?

1Building the objective function

The variables are the development spending \(x\) and the promotion spending \(y\), in thousands of euros. We build the profit in parts.

Profit per unit. Each unit sold brings \(50 - 30 = 20\) euros. The spending is in thousands of euros, so this amount is also expressed in thousands: 20 euros \(= 0.02\) thousand euros.

Profit from all units sold. \(N(x;\,y)\) units are sold, so they bring \(0.02\cdot N(x;\,y)\) thousand euros.

Spending. From this amount we subtract the development and promotion spending \(x\) and \(y\):

\[ P(x;\,y) = 0.02\cdot N(x;\,y) - x - y \]

We substitute \(N(x;\,y)\) and multiply: \(0.02\cdot 3200 = 64\), \(0.02\cdot 5000 = 100\):

\[ P(x;\,y) = 0.02\Big(\frac{3200x}{x + 4} + \frac{5000y}{y + 9}\Big) - x - y = \frac{64x}{x + 4} + \frac{100y}{y + 9} - x - y \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &P(x;\,y) \\ &= 0.02\Big(\frac{3200x}{x + 4} + \frac{5000y}{y + 9}\Big) - x - y \\ &= \frac{64x}{x + 4} + \frac{100y}{y + 9} - x - y \end{aligned} \]

2The first order partial derivatives

By the quotient rule

\[ \Big(\frac{x}{x + 4}\Big)'_x = \frac{1\cdot(x + 4) - x\cdot 1}{(x + 4)^2} = \frac{4}{(x + 4)^2} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \Big(\frac{x}{x + 4}\Big)'_x &= \frac{1\cdot(x + 4) - x\cdot 1}{(x + 4)^2} \\ &= \frac{4}{(x + 4)^2} \end{aligned} \]

so the derivative of the term \(\frac{64x}{x + 4}\) is \(\frac{64\cdot 4}{(x + 4)^2} = \frac{256}{(x + 4)^2}\). In the same way, the derivative of the term \(\frac{100y}{y + 9}\) is \(\frac{100\cdot 9}{(y + 9)^2} = \frac{900}{(y + 9)^2}\), and the derivatives of the terms \(-x\) and \(-y\) with respect to the corresponding variables are \(-1\):

\[ P'_x = \frac{256}{(x + 4)^2} - 1;\qquad P'_y = \frac{900}{(y + 9)^2} - 1 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} P'_x = \frac{256}{(x + 4)^2} - 1; \\ P'_y = \frac{900}{(y + 9)^2} - 1 \end{gathered} \]

Each derivative is the difference of a fraction and a number. To subtract, we need a common denominator, so we write the number 1 as a fraction with the same denominator: \(1 = \frac{(x + 4)^2}{(x + 4)^2}\). Then we subtract the numerators and keep the common denominator:

\[ P'_x = \frac{256}{(x + 4)^2} - \frac{(x + 4)^2}{(x + 4)^2} = \frac{256 - (x + 4)^2}{(x + 4)^2} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} P'_x &= \frac{256}{(x + 4)^2} - \frac{(x + 4)^2}{(x + 4)^2} \\ &= \frac{256 - (x + 4)^2}{(x + 4)^2} \end{aligned} \]

In the same way, \(1 = \frac{(y + 9)^2}{(y + 9)^2}\):

\[ P'_y = \frac{900}{(y + 9)^2} - \frac{(y + 9)^2}{(y + 9)^2} = \frac{900 - (y + 9)^2}{(y + 9)^2} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} P'_y &= \frac{900}{(y + 9)^2} - \frac{(y + 9)^2}{(y + 9)^2} \\ &= \frac{900 - (y + 9)^2}{(y + 9)^2} \end{aligned} \]

3The system of equations

We set both partial derivatives equal to zero:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} \frac{256 - (x + 4)^2}{(x + 4)^2} &= 0 \\ \frac{900 - (y + 9)^2}{(y + 9)^2} &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables are in the denominators and are squared. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

However, each equation contains only one variable, so we solve the equations separately. A fraction is equal to zero when its numerator is equal to zero and its denominator is not equal to zero. Therefore each equation is replaced by two conditions:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 256 - (x + 4)^2 &= 0 \\ (x + 4)^2 &\ne 0 \\ 900 - (y + 9)^2 &= 0 \\ (y + 9)^2 &\ne 0 \end{aligned} \right. \]

4Solving the system

The conditions on the denominators. A square is equal to zero only when the expression itself is equal to zero, so \((x + 4)^2 \ne 0\) when \(x \ne -4\), and \((y + 9)^2 \ne 0\) when \(y \ne -9\).

The first equation. We move the term \((x + 4)^2\) to the right-hand side and expand it by the formula \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\):

\[ (x + 4)^2 = 256 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 + 8x + 16 = 256 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 + 8x - 240 = 0 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} (x + 4)^2 = 256 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x^2 + 8x + 16 = 256 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x^2 + 8x - 240 = 0 \end{gathered} \]

We solve the quadratic equation obtained with the discriminant:

\[ D = 8^2 - 4\cdot 1\cdot(-240) = 64 + 960 = 1024;\qquad \sqrt{D} = 32 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} D = 8^2 - 4\cdot 1\cdot(-240) \\ = 64 + 960 = 1024 \\ \sqrt{D} = 32 \end{gathered} \]
\[ x_1 = \frac{-8 - 32}{2} = -20;\qquad x_2 = \frac{-8 + 32}{2} = 12 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x_1 = \frac{-8 - 32}{2} = -20; \\ x_2 = \frac{-8 + 32}{2} = 12 \end{gathered} \]

The spending cannot be negative, so we reject \(x = -20\). The solution \(x = 12\) is different from the excluded value \(-4\), so it is suitable.

The second equation is solved in the same way:

\[ (y + 9)^2 = 900 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^2 + 18y + 81 = 900 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^2 + 18y - 819 = 0 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} (y + 9)^2 = 900 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y^2 + 18y + 81 = 900 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y^2 + 18y - 819 = 0 \end{gathered} \]
\[ D = 18^2 - 4\cdot 1\cdot(-819) = 324 + 3276 = 3600;\qquad \sqrt{D} = 60 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} D = 18^2 - 4\cdot 1\cdot(-819) \\ = 324 + 3276 = 3600 \\ \sqrt{D} = 60 \end{gathered} \]
\[ y_1 = \frac{-18 - 60}{2} = -39;\qquad y_2 = \frac{-18 + 60}{2} = 21 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} y_1 = \frac{-18 - 60}{2} = -39; \\ y_2 = \frac{-18 + 60}{2} = 21 \end{gathered} \]

We reject the negative solution \(y = -39\), and \(y = 21\) is different from the excluded value \(-9\). We obtain the critical point \(K(12;\,21)\).

5The sufficient condition

We write the fraction as a power: \(\frac{256}{(x + 4)^2} = 256(x + 4)^{-2}\). Then \(\big(256(x + 4)^{-2}\big)'_x = -512(x + 4)^{-3}\). The derivative with respect to \(y\) is found in the same way:

\[ P''_{xx} = -\frac{512}{(x + 4)^3};\qquad P''_{xy} = 0;\qquad P''_{yy} = -\frac{1800}{(y + 9)^3} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} P''_{xx} = -\frac{512}{(x + 4)^3}; \\ P''_{xy} = 0; \\ P''_{yy} = -\frac{1800}{(y + 9)^3} \end{gathered} \]

The derivatives depend on \(x\) and \(y\), so we obtain \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) by substituting the coordinates of the critical point \(K(12;\,21)\), \(x = 12\) and \(y = 21\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{xx}(K) = -\frac{512}{(12 + 4)^3} = -\frac{512}{4096} = -0.125 \\ B &= P''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= P''_{yy}(K) = -\frac{1800}{(21 + 9)^3} = -\frac{1800}{27\,000} = -\frac{1}{15} \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-0.125)\cdot\Big(-\frac{1}{15}\Big) - 0^2 = \frac{1}{120} > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{xx}(K) = -\frac{512}{(12 + 4)^3} \\ &= -\frac{512}{4096} = -0.125 \\ B &= P''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= P''_{yy}(K) = -\frac{1800}{(21 + 9)^3} \\ &= -\frac{1800}{27\,000} = -\frac{1}{15} \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-0.125)\cdot\Big(-\frac{1}{15}\Big) - 0^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{120} > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K(12;\,21)\) is a maximum point.

6The sales and the maximum profit

\[ N(12;\,21) = \frac{3200\cdot 12}{16} + \frac{5000\cdot 21}{30} = 2400 + 3500 = 5900 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} N(12;\,21) &= \frac{3200\cdot 12}{16} + \frac{5000\cdot 21}{30} \\ &= 2400 + 3500 = 5900 \end{aligned} \]
\[ P_{\max}(12;\,21) = \frac{64\cdot 12}{16} + \frac{100\cdot 21}{30} - 12 - 21 = 48 + 70 - 12 - 21 = 85 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &P_{\max}(12;\,21) \\ &= \frac{64\cdot 12}{16} + \frac{100\cdot 21}{30} - 12 - 21 \\ &= 48 + 70 - 12 - 21 = 85 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The company should spend 12 thousand euros on development and 21 thousand euros on promotion. It would then sell 5900 units, and the maximum profit would be 85 thousand euros.

Example 4

Laying a cable through a forest and under a lake

Problem A telecommunications company lays a fibre-optic cable from its station S to a factory F. From the station the cable first crosses a forest strip 4 km wide, then runs along the bottom of a lake 2.4 km wide, and finally runs along the road on the far shore to the factory. The factory lies 10 km east of S (see the figure). Laying one kilometre of cable costs 50 thousand euros in the forest, 78 thousand euros under the lake and 30 thousand euros along the road. Let \(x\) be the distance east that the cable covers in the forest, and \(y\) the distance east that it covers under the lake.
Find \(x\) and \(y\) that minimize the cost of laying the cable. What is the minimum cost?

1The lengths of the parts of the route

The cable consists of three parts. We express the length of each of them through \(x\) and \(y\).

In the forest. The part is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. One leg is equal to the width of the forest, 4 km, and the other to the shift \(x\). By the Pythagorean theorem, its length is

\[ \sqrt{4^2 + x^2} = \sqrt{16 + x^2}\ \text{km} \]

Under the lake. The legs are the width of the lake, 2.4 km, and the shift \(y\). Since \(2.4^2 = 5.76\), the length is

\[ \sqrt{2.4^2 + y^2} = \sqrt{5.76 + y^2}\ \text{km} \]

Along the road. In total the cable has to cover 10 km to the east. In the forest and under the lake it moves \(x + y\) km to the east, so \(10 - x - y\) km remain along the road.

2The objective function

We multiply the length of each part by the cost of laying one kilometre and add the results. The cost, in thousands of euros, is

\[ TC(x;\,y) = 50\sqrt{16 + x^2} + 78\sqrt{5.76 + y^2} + 30(10 - x - y) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &TC(x;\,y) \\ &= 50\sqrt{16 + x^2} + 78\sqrt{5.76 + y^2} \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 30(10 - x - y) \end{aligned} \]

3The first order partial derivatives

By the chain rule,

\[ \big(\sqrt{16 + x^2}\big)'_x = \frac{2x}{2\sqrt{16 + x^2}} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} \]

The root with \(y\) is differentiated in the same way. The derivative of the last term \(30(10 - x - y)\) with respect to each variable is \(-30\):

\[ TC'_x = \frac{50x}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} - 30;\qquad TC'_y = \frac{78y}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}} - 30 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} TC'_x = \frac{50x}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} - 30; \\ TC'_y = \frac{78y}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}} - 30 \end{gathered} \]

Each derivative is the difference of a fraction and a number. We write the number 30 as a fraction with the same denominator, \(30 = \frac{30\sqrt{16 + x^2}}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}}\), and subtract the numerators:

\[ TC'_x = \frac{50x}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} - \frac{30\sqrt{16 + x^2}}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} = \frac{50x - 30\sqrt{16 + x^2}}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} TC'_x &= \frac{50x}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} - \frac{30\sqrt{16 + x^2}}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} \\ &= \frac{50x - 30\sqrt{16 + x^2}}{\sqrt{16 + x^2}} \end{aligned} \]

In the same way, \(30 = \frac{30\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}}\):

\[ TC'_y = \frac{78y}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}} - \frac{30\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}} = \frac{78y - 30\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} TC'_y &= \frac{78y}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}} - \frac{30\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}} \\ &= \frac{78y - 30\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}}{\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}} \end{aligned} \]

4The system of equations

We set both partial derivatives equal to zero and replace each equation by two conditions: the numerator is equal to zero, and the denominator is not equal to zero:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 50x - 30\sqrt{16 + x^2} &= 0 \\ \sqrt{16 + x^2} &\ne 0 \\ 78y - 30\sqrt{5.76 + y^2} &= 0 \\ \sqrt{5.76 + y^2} &\ne 0 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables are under roots. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

The expressions \(16 + x^2\) and \(5.76 + y^2\) are always positive, so their square roots are not equal to zero – the conditions on the denominators always hold. Each equation contains only one variable, so we solve the equations separately.

5Solving the system

The first equation. We move the term with the root to the right-hand side and divide both sides by 10:

\[ 50x = 30\sqrt{16 + x^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; 5x = 3\sqrt{16 + x^2} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 50x = 30\sqrt{16 + x^2} \\ \Longrightarrow\; 5x = 3\sqrt{16 + x^2} \end{gathered} \]

To get rid of the root, we square both sides: \((5x)^2 = 25x^2\), \(\big(3\sqrt{16 + x^2}\big)^2 = 9(16 + x^2)\). Then we expand the brackets and collect like terms:

\[ 25x^2 = 9(16 + x^2) \;\Longrightarrow\; 25x^2 = 144 + 9x^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 16x^2 = 144 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 = 9 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 25x^2 = 9(16 + x^2) \\ \Longrightarrow\; 25x^2 = 144 + 9x^2 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 16x^2 = 144 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 = 9 \end{gathered} \]

We have obtained an incomplete quadratic equation. We move 9 to the left-hand side and factor by the difference of squares formula:

\[ x^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 3)(x + 3) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3\ \text{or}\ x = -3 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 3)(x + 3) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 3\ \text{or}\ x = -3 \end{gathered} \]

Squaring an equation may produce extraneous solutions, so we check both of them in the original equation \(5x = 3\sqrt{16 + x^2}\). Its right-hand side is positive, so the left-hand side \(5x\) must also be positive. Only \(x = 3\) is suitable: \(5\cdot 3 = 15\) and \(3\sqrt{16 + 9} = 3\cdot 5 = 15\).

The second equation is solved in the same way. We move the term with the root to the right-hand side, divide both sides by 6 and square them:

\[ 78y = 30\sqrt{5.76 + y^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; 13y = 5\sqrt{5.76 + y^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; 169y^2 = 25(5.76 + y^2) \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 78y = 30\sqrt{5.76 + y^2} \\ \Longrightarrow\; 13y = 5\sqrt{5.76 + y^2} \\ \Longrightarrow\; 169y^2 = 25(5.76 + y^2) \end{gathered} \]
\[ 169y^2 = 144 + 25y^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 144y^2 = 144 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^2 = 1 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 169y^2 = 144 + 25y^2 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 144y^2 = 144 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^2 = 1 \end{gathered} \]

Again we have obtained an incomplete quadratic equation, and we factor it:

\[ y^2 - 1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 1)(y + 1) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 1\ \text{or}\ y = -1 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} y^2 - 1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 1)(y + 1) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 1\ \text{or}\ y = -1 \end{gathered} \]

In the original equation \(13y = 5\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}\) only the positive solution \(y = 1\) is suitable: \(13\cdot 1 = 13\) and \(5\sqrt{6.76} = 5\cdot 2.6 = 13\). We obtain the critical point \(K(3;\,1)\).

6The sufficient condition

We use the product rule and the chain rule:

\[ TC''_{xx} = \frac{50\cdot 16}{\big(\sqrt{16 + x^2}\big)^3};\qquad TC''_{xy} = 0;\qquad TC''_{yy} = \frac{78\cdot 5.76}{\big(\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}\big)^3} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} TC''_{xx} = \frac{50\cdot 16}{\big(\sqrt{16 + x^2}\big)^3} \\ TC''_{xy} = 0 \\ TC''_{yy} = \frac{78\cdot 5.76}{\big(\sqrt{5.76 + y^2}\big)^3} \end{gathered} \]

We substitute the coordinates of the critical point \(K(3;\,1)\), \(x = 3\) and \(y = 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= TC''_{xx}(K) = \frac{50\cdot 16}{\big(\sqrt{16 + 3^2}\big)^3} = \frac{800}{5^3} = 6.4 \\ B &= TC''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= TC''_{yy}(K) = \frac{78\cdot 5.76}{\big(\sqrt{5.76 + 1^2}\big)^3} = \frac{449.28}{2.6^3} \approx 25.56 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 6.4\cdot 25.56 - 0^2 \approx 163.6 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= TC''_{xx}(K) \\ &= \frac{50\cdot 16}{\big(\sqrt{16 + 3^2}\big)^3} = \frac{800}{5^3} \\ &= 6.4 \\ B &= TC''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= TC''_{yy}(K) \\ &= \frac{78\cdot 5.76}{\big(\sqrt{5.76 + 1^2}\big)^3} \\ &= \frac{449.28}{2.6^3} \approx 25.56 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= 6.4\cdot 25.56 - 0^2 \\ &\approx 163.6 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A > 0\), so \(K(3;\,1)\) is a minimum point.

7The minimum cost

The length of the cable in the forest is \(\sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 5\) km, under the lake \(\sqrt{5.76 + 1} = \sqrt{6.76} = 2.6\) km, and along the road \(10 - 3 - 1 = 6\) km:

\[ TC_{\min}(3;\,1) = 50\cdot 5 + 78\cdot 2.6 + 30\cdot 6 = 250 + 202.8 + 180 = 632.8 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &TC_{\min}(3;\,1) \\ &= 50\cdot 5 + 78\cdot 2.6 + 30\cdot 6 \\ &= 250 + 202.8 + 180 = 632.8 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The cable should leave the forest 3 km east of S and reach the road 1 km further east. The minimum cost of laying the cable is 632.8 thousand euros.

Example 5

Heating and cooling costs of a warehouse

Problem A warehouse in the shape of a rectangular box is to have a volume of 15 000 cubic metres. Its yearly heating and cooling costs are estimated at 7 euros per square metre of the roof, 3 euros per square metre of the floor, 6 euros per square metre of the front and back walls, which have large windows, and 4 euros per square metre of the side walls.
Find the dimensions of the warehouse that minimize the yearly heating and cooling cost. What is the minimum cost?

1The costs of the surfaces

We denote the width of the front wall by \(x\), the depth of the warehouse by \(y\) and its height by \(z\) (in metres). The area of each surface is multiplied by the cost per square metre:

SurfaceAreaEUR/m²Costs
Roof\(xy\)\(7\)\(7xy\)
Floor\(xy\)\(3\)\(3xy\)
Front and back walls\(2xz\)\(6\)\(12xz\)
Side walls\(2yz\)\(4\)\(8yz\)

Adding them, we get

\[ TC = 7xy + 3xy + 12xz + 8yz = 10xy + 12xz + 8yz \]
\[ \begin{aligned} TC &= 7xy + 3xy + 12xz + 8yz \\ &= 10xy + 12xz + 8yz \end{aligned} \]

2Eliminating the third variable

The cost depends on three variables, but they are connected by the volume: \(xyz = 15\,000\). From this equation we express the height by dividing both sides by \(xy\):

\[ z = \frac{15\,000}{xy} \]

We substitute this expression in place of \(z\). In the product \(12x\cdot\frac{15\,000}{xy}\) the \(x\) cancels, and in the product \(8y\cdot\frac{15\,000}{xy}\) the \(y\) cancels:

\[ TC(x;\,y) = 10xy + 12x\cdot\frac{15\,000}{xy} + 8y\cdot\frac{15\,000}{xy} = 10xy + \frac{180\,000}{y} + \frac{120\,000}{x} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &TC(x;\,y) \\ &= 10xy + 12x\cdot\frac{15\,000}{xy} + 8y\cdot\frac{15\,000}{xy} \\ &= 10xy + \frac{180\,000}{y} + \frac{120\,000}{x} \end{aligned} \]

Now the objective function depends on two variables only.

3The first order partial derivatives

We write the fractions as powers with negative exponents, for example \(\frac{120\,000}{x} = 120\,000x^{-1}\), so \(\big(120\,000x^{-1}\big)'_x = -120\,000x^{-2} = -\frac{120\,000}{x^2}\). For convenience, we write each derivative as a single fraction:

\[ \begin{aligned} TC'_x &= 10y - \frac{120\,000}{x^2} = \frac{10x^2y - 120\,000}{x^2} \\ TC'_y &= 10x - \frac{180\,000}{y^2} = \frac{10xy^2 - 180\,000}{y^2} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} TC'_x &= 10y - \frac{120\,000}{x^2} \\ &= \frac{10x^2y - 120\,000}{x^2} \\ TC'_y &= 10x - \frac{180\,000}{y^2} \\ &= \frac{10xy^2 - 180\,000}{y^2} \end{aligned} \]

4The system of equations

The dimensions are positive, so the denominators \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) are not equal to zero. We set the numerators equal to zero, move the numbers to the right-hand side and divide both equations by 10:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 10x^2y - 120\,000 &= 0 \\ 10xy^2 - 180\,000 &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \;\Longrightarrow\; \left\{ \begin{aligned} x^2y &= 12\,000 \\ xy^2 &= 18\,000 \end{aligned} \right. \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \left\{ \begin{aligned} 10x^2y - 120\,000 &= 0 \\ 10xy^2 - 180\,000 &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \\ \Longrightarrow\; \left\{ \begin{aligned} x^2y &= 12\,000 \\ xy^2 &= 18\,000 \end{aligned} \right. \end{gathered} \]

This system is not linear: the variables are multiplied by each other and raised to powers. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

5Solving the system by dividing the equations

Both equations contain the same variables, only in different powers. Therefore we divide the second equation by the first: the left-hand side by the left-hand side and the right-hand side by the right-hand side. Powers of the same base cancel: \(\frac{x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{x}\), \(\frac{y^2}{y} = y\):

\[ \frac{xy^2}{x^2y} = \frac{18\,000}{12\,000} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{y}{x} = 1.5 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 1.5x \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{xy^2}{x^2y} = \frac{18\,000}{12\,000} \\ \Longrightarrow\; \frac{y}{x} = 1.5 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 1.5x \end{gathered} \]

We substitute the expression into the first equation in place of \(y\):

\[ x^2\cdot 1.5x = 12\,000 \;\Longrightarrow\; 1.5x^3 = 12\,000 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^3 = 8000 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \sqrt[3]{8000} = 20 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2\cdot 1.5x = 12\,000 \;\Longrightarrow\; 1.5x^3 = 12\,000 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x^3 = 8000 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \sqrt[3]{8000} = 20 \end{gathered} \]

We substitute \(x\) into \(y = 1.5x\): \(y = 1.5\cdot 20 = 30\). We obtain the critical point \(K(20;\,30)\).

6The sufficient condition

The second order partial derivatives:

\[ TC''_{xx} = \frac{240\,000}{x^3};\qquad TC''_{xy} = 10;\qquad TC''_{yy} = \frac{360\,000}{y^3} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} TC''_{xx} = \frac{240\,000}{x^3}; \\ TC''_{xy} = 10; \\ TC''_{yy} = \frac{360\,000}{y^3} \end{gathered} \]

The derivatives \(TC''_{xx}\) and \(TC''_{yy}\) depend on the variables, so we substitute the coordinates of the critical point \(K(20;\,30)\), \(x = 20\) and \(y = 30\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= TC''_{xx}(K) = \frac{240\,000}{20^3} = \frac{240\,000}{8000} = 30 \\ B &= TC''_{xy}(K) = 10 \\ C &= TC''_{yy}(K) = \frac{360\,000}{30^3} = \frac{360\,000}{27\,000} = \frac{40}{3} \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 30\cdot\frac{40}{3} - 10^2 = 400 - 100 = 300 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= TC''_{xx}(K) = \frac{240\,000}{20^3} \\ &= \frac{240\,000}{8000} = 30 \\ B &= TC''_{xy}(K) = 10 \\ C &= TC''_{yy}(K) = \frac{360\,000}{30^3} \\ &= \frac{360\,000}{27\,000} = \frac{40}{3} \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 30\cdot\frac{40}{3} - 10^2 \\ &= 400 - 100 = 300 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A > 0\), so \(K(20;\,30)\) is a minimum point.

7The height and the minimum cost

\[ z = \frac{15\,000}{20\cdot 30} = 25 \]
\[ TC_{\min}(20;\,30) = 10\cdot 600 + \frac{180\,000}{30} + \frac{120\,000}{20} = 6000 + 6000 + 6000 = 18\,000 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &TC_{\min}(20;\,30) \\ &= 10\cdot 600 + \frac{180\,000}{30} + \frac{120\,000}{20} \\ &= 6000 + 6000 + 6000 = 18\,000 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The warehouse should be 20 metres wide, 30 metres deep and 25 metres high. The minimum yearly heating and cooling cost is 18 000 euros.

Example 6

Working conditions in a call centre

Problem A call centre tests how two working conditions affect its employees. The level of background music is denoted by \(x\), and the length of additional breaks by \(y\). Both are measured on a scale from 0 to 5. Quiet music and short breaks help, but music that is too loud or breaks that are too long make it harder to concentrate. The number of additional calls that an employee handles per day is \[ E(x;\,y) = 40xye^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)} \] Find the critical points with \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\) and classify them. At what levels is the number of additional calls largest?

1The first order partial derivatives

When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the factor \(40y\) is treated as a constant. For the product \(x\cdot e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}\) we use the product rule, and by the chain rule the derivative of the exponential function is \(-0.25x\cdot e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}\). We take the common factor \(e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}\) out of the brackets:

\[ E'_x = 40y\big(e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)} - 0.25x^2e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}\big) = 40ye^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}(1 - 0.25x^2) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} E'_x &= 40y\big(e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)} \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- 0.25x^2e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}\big) \\ &= 40ye^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}(1 - 0.25x^2) \end{aligned} \]

In the same way,

\[ E'_y = 40xe^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}(1 - 0.25y^2) \]

2The system of equations

We set both partial derivatives equal to zero. The exponential function is always positive, so both equations can be divided by \(40e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}\) – this does not change the solutions:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} y(1 - 0.25x^2) &= 0 \\ x(1 - 0.25y^2) &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variables are multiplied by each other, and \(x\) and \(y\) are squared. Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

The left-hand side of each equation is a product. A product is equal to zero when at least one of its factors is equal to zero, so we consider cases. An equation must not be divided by a variable that may be equal to zero – solutions would be lost in this way.

3Solving the system by cases

The first equation holds when \(y = 0\) or when \(1 - 0.25x^2 = 0\).

Case 1: \(y = 0\). Substituting into the second equation gives \(x(1 - 0) = 0\), i.e. \(x = 0\). This gives the point \((0;\,0)\).

Case 2: \(1 - 0.25x^2 = 0\). Multiplying both sides by \(-4\), we get an incomplete quadratic equation, which we factor:

\[ x^2 - 4 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 2)(x + 2) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 2\ \text{or}\ x = -2 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 - 4 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 2)(x + 2) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 2\ \text{or}\ x = -2 \end{gathered} \]

Since \(x \ge 0\), \(x = 2\) is suitable. Substituting into the second equation gives \(2(1 - 0.25y^2) = 0\), so \(1 - 0.25y^2 = 0\). We solve it in the same way:

\[ y^2 - 4 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 2)(y + 2) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 2\ \text{or}\ y = -2 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} y^2 - 4 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 2)(y + 2) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 2\ \text{or}\ y = -2 \end{gathered} \]

Since \(y \ge 0\), \(y = 2\) is suitable. This gives the point \((2;\,2)\).

There are two critical points. We label them \(K_1(0;\,0)\) and \(K_2(2;\,2)\).

4The sufficient condition

We denote \(g = e^{-0.125(x^2 + y^2)}\) and differentiate the first order derivatives once more:

\[ \begin{gathered} E''_{xx} = yg(2.5x^3 - 30x);\qquad E''_{yy} = xg(2.5y^3 - 30y) \\ E''_{xy} = 40g(1 - 0.25x^2)(1 - 0.25y^2) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} E''_{xx} &= yg(2.5x^3 - 30x) \\ E''_{xy} &= 40g(1 - 0.25x^2)(1 - 0.25y^2) \\ E''_{yy} &= xg(2.5y^3 - 30y) \end{aligned} \]

At the point \(K_1(0;\,0)\) we get \(g = e^{0} = 1\), and at the point \(K_2(2;\,2)\) – \(g = e^{-0.125\cdot 8} = e^{-1}\).

The point \(K_1(0;\,0)\). We substitute \(x = 0\), \(y = 0\) and \(g = 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= E''_{xx}(K_1) = 0\cdot 1\cdot(2.5\cdot 0^3 - 30\cdot 0) = 0 \\ B &= E''_{xy}(K_1) = 40\cdot 1\cdot(1 - 0)(1 - 0) = 40 \\ C &= E''_{yy}(K_1) = 0\cdot 1\cdot(2.5\cdot 0^3 - 30\cdot 0) = 0 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 0\cdot 0 - 40^2 = -1600 < 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= E''_{xx}(K_1) \\ &= 0\cdot 1\cdot(2.5\cdot 0^3 - 30\cdot 0) \\ &= 0 \\ B &= E''_{xy}(K_1) \\ &= 40\cdot 1\cdot(1 - 0)(1 - 0) = 40 \\ C &= E''_{yy}(K_1) \\ &= 0\cdot 1\cdot(2.5\cdot 0^3 - 30\cdot 0) \\ &= 0 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 0\cdot 0 - 40^2 \\ &= -1600 < 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D < 0\), so \(K_1\) is a saddle point.

The point \(K_2(2;\,2)\). We substitute \(x = 2\), \(y = 2\) and \(g = e^{-1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= E''_{xx}(K_2) = 2e^{-1}(2.5\cdot 2^3 - 30\cdot 2) = -80e^{-1} \\ B &= E''_{xy}(K_2) = 40e^{-1}(1 - 0.25\cdot 2^2)(1 - 0.25\cdot 2^2) = 0 \\ C &= E''_{yy}(K_2) = 2e^{-1}(2.5\cdot 2^3 - 30\cdot 2) = -80e^{-1} \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-80e^{-1})^2 - 0^2 = 6400e^{-2} > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= E''_{xx}(K_2) \\ &= 2e^{-1}(2.5\cdot 2^3 - 30\cdot 2) \\ &= -80e^{-1} \\ B &= E''_{xy}(K_2) \\ &= 40e^{-1}(1 - 0.25\cdot 2^2)(1 - 0.25\cdot 2^2) \\ &= 0 \\ C &= E''_{yy}(K_2) \\ &= 2e^{-1}(2.5\cdot 2^3 - 30\cdot 2) \\ &= -80e^{-1} \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-80e^{-1})^2 - 0^2 \\ &= 6400e^{-2} > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K_2\) is a maximum point.

5The largest value

\[ E_{\max}(2;\,2) = 40\cdot 2\cdot 2\cdot e^{-1} = 160e^{-1} \approx 58.86 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} E_{\max}(2;\,2) &= 40\cdot 2\cdot 2\cdot e^{-1} \\ &= 160e^{-1} \approx 58.86 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The point \(K_1(0;\,0)\) is a saddle point in the whole plane, and in the admissible region the function has its smallest value there. The number of additional calls is largest when both the music level and the length of the additional breaks are 2 points on the scale. An employee then handles approximately 58.86 additional calls per day.

Example 7

Temperature and fertiliser in a greenhouse

Problem In a heated greenhouse the revenue from tomatoes, in euros per square metre, is \[ R = 10T\big(1 - e^{-0.5x}\big) \] where \(T\) is the temperature maintained in the greenhouse (in degrees Celsius) and \(x\) is the amount of fertiliser (in kilograms per square metre). The fertiliser costs 16 euros per kilogram, and the heating costs \(0.25T^2\) euros per square metre.
Find the temperature and the amount of fertiliser that maximize the profit per square metre. What is the maximum profit?

1Building the objective function

The variables are the temperature \(T\) and the amount of fertiliser \(x\). The profit per square metre is the revenue minus the costs.

The revenue is given: \(R = 10T\big(1 - e^{-0.5x}\big)\).

Costs are of two kinds. Fertiliser: \(x\) kilograms at 16 euros cost \(16x\) euros. Heating costs \(0.25T^2\) euros.

We subtract both parts of the costs from the revenue:

\[ P(T;\,x) = 10T\big(1 - e^{-0.5x}\big) - 16x - 0.25T^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned} P(T;\,x) &= 10T\big(1 - e^{-0.5x}\big) \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- 16x - 0.25T^2 \end{aligned} \]

2The first order partial derivatives

When differentiating with respect to \(T\), the expression in brackets is a constant factor. When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the factor \(10T\) is constant, and \(\big(e^{-0.5x}\big)'_x = -0.5e^{-0.5x}\):

\[ P'_T = 10\big(1 - e^{-0.5x}\big) - 0.5T;\qquad P'_x = 5Te^{-0.5x} - 16 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} P'_T = 10\big(1 - e^{-0.5x}\big) - 0.5T; \\ P'_x = 5Te^{-0.5x} - 16 \end{gathered} \]

3The system of equations

We set both partial derivatives equal to zero:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 10\big(1 - e^{-0.5x}\big) - 0.5T &= 0 \\ 5Te^{-0.5x} - 16 &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \]

This system is not linear: the variable \(x\) is in the exponent of an exponential function, and in the second equation \(T\) is multiplied by \(e^{-0.5x}\). Therefore Gaussian elimination cannot be used for it – it applies only to systems of linear equations.

Both equations contain the same exponential expression, so we replace it by a new variable \(u = e^{-0.5x}\). The exponential function is always positive, so \(u > 0\). The system becomes simpler:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 10(1 - u) - 0.5T &= 0 \\ 5Tu - 16 &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \]

4Solving the system by substitution

We express \(T\) from the first equation. We move the term \(-0.5T\) to the right-hand side and multiply both sides by 2:

\[ 10(1 - u) = 0.5T \;\Longrightarrow\; T = 20(1 - u) \]

We substitute the expression into the second equation in place of \(T\) and expand the brackets:

\[ 5\cdot 20(1 - u)u - 16 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 100u - 100u^2 - 16 = 0 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 5\cdot 20(1 - u)u - 16 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; 100u - 100u^2 - 16 = 0 \end{gathered} \]

We multiply both sides by \(-1\) and divide them by 4. This gives a quadratic equation in the unknown \(u\):

\[ 25u^2 - 25u + 4 = 0 \]

We solve it with the discriminant:

\[ D = (-25)^2 - 4\cdot 25\cdot 4 = 625 - 400 = 225;\qquad \sqrt{D} = 15 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} D = (-25)^2 - 4\cdot 25\cdot 4 \\ = 625 - 400 = 225 \\ \sqrt{D} = 15 \end{gathered} \]
\[ u_1 = \frac{25 - 15}{50} = 0.2;\qquad u_2 = \frac{25 + 15}{50} = 0.8 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} u_1 = \frac{25 - 15}{50} = 0.2; \\ u_2 = \frac{25 + 15}{50} = 0.8 \end{gathered} \]

Both values are positive, so both are suitable. Each of them gives one critical point.

5Returning to \(x\) and \(T\)

Since \(u = e^{-0.5x}\), we solve the equation \(e^{-0.5x} = u\). We take the logarithm of both sides and use the rule \(\ln\big(a^n\big) = n\ln a\) and \(\ln e = 1\):

\[ \ln e^{-0.5x} = \ln u \;\Longrightarrow\; -0.5x\cdot\ln e = \ln u \;\Longrightarrow\; -0.5x = \ln u \;\Longrightarrow\; x = -2\ln u \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \ln e^{-0.5x} = \ln u \\ \Longrightarrow\; -0.5x\cdot\ln e = \ln u \\ \Longrightarrow\; -0.5x = \ln u \;\Longrightarrow\; x = -2\ln u \end{gathered} \]

When \(u = 0.2\). Since \(0.2 = \frac{1}{5} = 5^{-1}\), we get \(\ln 0.2 = -\ln 5\). We find the temperature from \(T = 20(1 - u)\):

\[ x = -2\ln 0.2 = 2\ln 5 \approx 3.22;\qquad T = 20\cdot(1 - 0.2) = 16 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x = -2\ln 0.2 = 2\ln 5 \approx 3.22; \\ T = 20\cdot(1 - 0.2) = 16 \end{gathered} \]

When \(u = 0.8\). Since \(0.8 = \frac{1}{1.25} = 1.25^{-1}\), we get \(\ln 0.8 = -\ln 1.25\):

\[ x = -2\ln 0.8 = 2\ln 1.25 \approx 0.45;\qquad T = 20\cdot(1 - 0.8) = 4 \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x = -2\ln 0.8 = 2\ln 1.25 \approx 0.45; \\ T = 20\cdot(1 - 0.8) = 4 \end{gathered} \]

There are two critical points \((T;\,x)\). We label them \(K_1(16;\,2\ln 5)\) and \(K_2(4;\,2\ln 1.25)\).

6The sufficient condition

\[ P''_{TT} = -0.5;\qquad P''_{Tx} = 5e^{-0.5x};\qquad P''_{xx} = -2.5Te^{-0.5x} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} P''_{TT} = -0.5; \\ P''_{Tx} = 5e^{-0.5x}; \\ P''_{xx} = -2.5Te^{-0.5x} \end{gathered} \]

The derivatives \(P''_{Tx}\) and \(P''_{xx}\) depend on the variables. The value \(e^{-0.5x} = u\) at each point is already known.

The point \(K_1(16;\,2\ln 5)\). We substitute \(T = 16\) and \(e^{-0.5x} = 0.2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{TT}(K_1) = -0.5 \\ B &= P''_{Tx}(K_1) = 5\cdot 0.2 = 1 \\ C &= P''_{xx}(K_1) = -2.5\cdot 16\cdot 0.2 = -8 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-0.5)\cdot(-8) - 1^2 = 4 - 1 = 3 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{TT}(K_1) = -0.5 \\ B &= P''_{Tx}(K_1) = 5\cdot 0.2 = 1 \\ C &= P''_{xx}(K_1) = -2.5\cdot 16\cdot 0.2 \\ &= -8 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-0.5)\cdot(-8) - 1^2 = 4 - 1 \\ &= 3 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A < 0\), so \(K_1\) is a maximum point.

The point \(K_2(4;\,2\ln 1.25)\). We substitute \(T = 4\) and \(e^{-0.5x} = 0.8\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{TT}(K_2) = -0.5 \\ B &= P''_{Tx}(K_2) = 5\cdot 0.8 = 4 \\ C &= P''_{xx}(K_2) = -2.5\cdot 4\cdot 0.8 = -8 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = (-0.5)\cdot(-8) - 4^2 = 4 - 16 = -12 < 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= P''_{TT}(K_2) = -0.5 \\ B &= P''_{Tx}(K_2) = 5\cdot 0.8 = 4 \\ C &= P''_{xx}(K_2) = -2.5\cdot 4\cdot 0.8 \\ &= -8 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= (-0.5)\cdot(-8) - 4^2 = 4 - 16 \\ &= -12 < 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D < 0\), so \(K_2\) is a saddle point.

7The maximum profit

At the point \(K_1(16;\,2\ln 5)\) we have \(e^{-0.5x} = 0.2\), so \(1 - e^{-0.5x} = 0.8\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\max} &= 10\cdot 16\cdot 0.8 - 16\cdot 2\ln 5 - 0.25\cdot 16^2 \\ &= 128 - 32\ln 5 - 64 = 64 - 32\ln 5 \approx 12.50 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\max} &= 10\cdot 16\cdot 0.8 - 16\cdot 2\ln 5 \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- 0.25\cdot 16^2 \\ &= 128 - 32\ln 5 - 64 \\ &= 64 - 32\ln 5 \approx 12.50 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

The profit is largest at a temperature of 16 degrees Celsius with approximately 3.22 kilograms of fertiliser per square metre. The maximum profit is approximately 12.50 euros per square metre.

A water company lays a pipeline from its pumping station S to a town T, which lies 15 km east of S. From S the pipeline first crosses a marsh 6 km wide, then a forest 3 km wide, and then runs along a road to T. Laying one kilometre costs 60 thousand euros in the marsh, 45 thousand euros in the forest and 36 thousand euros along the road. Let \(x\) and \(y\) be the distances east that the pipeline covers in the marsh and in the forest.

Find \(x\) and \(y\) that minimize the cost of laying the pipeline. What is the minimum cost?

The objective function:

\[ TC(x;\,y) = 60\sqrt{36 + x^2} + 45\sqrt{9 + y^2} + 36(15 - x - y) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &TC(x;\,y) \\ &= 60\sqrt{36 + x^2} + 45\sqrt{9 + y^2} \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}+ 36(15 - x - y) \end{aligned} \]

The derivatives with a common denominator and the system of equations:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} \frac{60x - 36\sqrt{36 + x^2}}{\sqrt{36 + x^2}} &= 0 \\ \frac{45y - 36\sqrt{9 + y^2}}{\sqrt{9 + y^2}} &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \;\Longrightarrow\; \left\{ \begin{aligned} 5x &= 3\sqrt{36 + x^2} \\ 5y &= 4\sqrt{9 + y^2} \end{aligned} \right. \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \left\{ \begin{aligned} \frac{60x - 36\sqrt{36 + x^2}}{\sqrt{36 + x^2}} &= 0 \\ \frac{45y - 36\sqrt{9 + y^2}}{\sqrt{9 + y^2}} &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \\ \Longrightarrow\; \left\{ \begin{aligned} 5x &= 3\sqrt{36 + x^2} \\ 5y &= 4\sqrt{9 + y^2} \end{aligned} \right. \end{gathered} \]

Squaring both equations gives incomplete quadratic equations; only the positive solutions are suitable:

\[ \begin{gathered} 4x^2 - 81 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (2x - 9)(2x + 9) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 4.5 \\ y^2 - 16 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 4)(y + 4) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 4 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 4x^2 - 81 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; (2x - 9)(2x + 9) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 4.5 \\[4pt] y^2 - 16 = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; (y - 4)(y + 4) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 4 \end{gathered} \]

The sufficient condition:

The second order derivatives: \(TC''_{xx} = \frac{60\cdot 36}{\big(\sqrt{36 + x^2}\big)^3}\), \(TC''_{xy} = 0\), \(TC''_{yy} = \frac{45\cdot 9}{\big(\sqrt{9 + y^2}\big)^3}\). We substitute the coordinates of the critical point \(K(4.5;\,4)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= TC''_{xx}(K) = \frac{60\cdot 36}{\big(\sqrt{36 + 4.5^2}\big)^3} = \frac{2160}{7.5^3} = 5.12 \\ B &= TC''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= TC''_{yy}(K) = \frac{45\cdot 9}{\big(\sqrt{9 + 4^2}\big)^3} = \frac{405}{5^3} = 3.24 \\ D &= AC - B^2 = 5.12\cdot 3.24 - 0^2 \approx 16.59 > 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= TC''_{xx}(K) \\ &= \frac{60\cdot 36}{\big(\sqrt{36 + 4.5^2}\big)^3} \\ &= \frac{2160}{7.5^3} = 5.12 \\ B &= TC''_{xy}(K) = 0 \\ C &= TC''_{yy}(K) \\ &= \frac{45\cdot 9}{\big(\sqrt{9 + 4^2}\big)^3} = \frac{405}{5^3} \\ &= 3.24 \\ D &= AC - B^2 \\ &= 5.12\cdot 3.24 - 0^2 \\ &\approx 16.59 > 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D > 0\) and \(A > 0\), so \(K(4.5;\,4)\) is a minimum point.

Answer. The pipeline should leave the marsh 4.5 km east of S and reach the road 4 km further east. The parts of the pipeline are 7.5 km long in the marsh, 5 km in the forest and 6.5 km along the road. The minimum cost is \(TC_{\min} = 909\) thousand euros.

A restaurant tests two ways of attracting guests: \(x\) hours of live music and \(y\) tasting events a week. A little of both attracts guests, but too much no longer brings more of them. The number of additional guests a week is

\[ E(x;\,y) = 30xye^{-\frac{x^2 + y^2}{18}} \]

Find the critical points with \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\) and classify them. At what values is the number of additional guests largest?

The derivatives and the system of equations:

\[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} 30ye^{-\frac{x^2 + y^2}{18}}\Big(1 - \frac{x^2}{9}\Big) &= 0 \\ 30xe^{-\frac{x^2 + y^2}{18}}\Big(1 - \frac{y^2}{9}\Big) &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \;\Longrightarrow\; K_1(0;\,0),\quad K_2(3;\,3) \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \left\{ \begin{aligned} 30ye^{-\frac{x^2 + y^2}{18}}\Big(1 - \frac{x^2}{9}\Big) &= 0 \\ 30xe^{-\frac{x^2 + y^2}{18}}\Big(1 - \frac{y^2}{9}\Big) &= 0 \end{aligned} \right. \\ \Longrightarrow\; K_1(0;\,0),\quad K_2(3;\,3) \end{gathered} \]

The factor \(30e^{-\frac{x^2 + y^2}{18}}\) is never equal to zero. If \(y = 0\), the second equation gives \(x = 0\), i.e. the point \((0;\,0)\). \[ \begin{gathered} x^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 3)(x + 3) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3 \\ y^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 3)(y + 3) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 3 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} x^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 3)(x + 3) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; x = 3 \\[4pt] y^2 - 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (y - 3)(y + 3) = 0 \\ \Longrightarrow\; y = 3 \end{gathered} \]

We denote \(g = e^{-\frac{x^2 + y^2}{18}}\). The second order derivatives:

\[ \begin{gathered} E''_{xx} = 30yg\Big(\frac{x^3}{81} - \frac{x}{3}\Big);\quad E''_{yy} = 30xg\Big(\frac{y^3}{81} - \frac{y}{3}\Big) \\ E''_{xy} = 30g\Big(1 - \frac{x^2}{9}\Big)\Big(1 - \frac{y^2}{9}\Big) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} E''_{xx} &= 30yg\Big(\frac{x^3}{81} - \frac{x}{3}\Big) \\ E''_{xy} &= 30g\Big(1 - \frac{x^2}{9}\Big)\Big(1 - \frac{y^2}{9}\Big) \\ E''_{yy} &= 30xg\Big(\frac{y^3}{81} - \frac{y}{3}\Big) \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= E''_{xx}(K_1) = 0 \\ B &= E''_{xy}(K_1) = 30\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot 1 = 30 \\ C &= E''_{yy}(K_1) = 0 \\ D &= 0\cdot 0 - 30^2 = -900 < 0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} A &= E''_{xx}(K_1) = 0 \\ B &= E''_{xy}(K_1) \\ &= 30\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot 1 = 30 \\ C &= E''_{yy}(K_1) = 0 \\ D &= 0\cdot 0 - 30^2 = -900 < 0 \end{aligned} \]

\(D < 0\), so \(K_1\) is a saddle point in the whole plane.

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= E''_{xx}(K_2) = 90e^{-1}\Big(\frac{27}{81} - 1\Big) = -60e^{-1} \\ B &= E''_{xy}(K_2) = 0 \\ C &= E''_{yy}(K_2) = -60e^{-1} \\ D &= 3600e^{-2} > 0 \end{aligned} \]
