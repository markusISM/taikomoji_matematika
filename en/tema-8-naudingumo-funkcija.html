The Utility Function and the Marginal Rate of Substitution

This section looks at how the satisfaction of a consumer depends on the amounts of two goods. The partial derivatives of the utility function – the marginal utilities – show how the utility changes, and the marginal rate of substitution shows how much of one good the consumer is willing to give up for an additional unit of the other good.

The utility function

A consumer decides how much of each good to buy. Different combinations bring different satisfaction: two cups of coffee and one sandwich, or one cup of coffee and two sandwiches. To compare such combinations, economists describe the satisfaction of the consumer by a number called utility.

A combination of \(x\) units of the first good and \(y\) units of the second good is called a consumption basket and is written \((x;\,y)\).

The utility function \(U = U(x;\,y)\) assigns to every possible consumption basket a value that describes the satisfaction of the consumer; here \(x\) and \(y\) are the amounts of consumption of the two goods.

The value of the utility function is used to compare baskets. The consumer prefers the basket with the higher utility. Two baskets with the same utility are equally good for the consumer, so it does not matter which one is chosen. For example, with \(U(x;\,y) = xy\) the baskets \(A = (4;\,9)\) and \(B = (6;\,6)\) both give \(U = 36\) and are equally good. The basket \((5;\,8)\) gives \(U = 40\), so it is preferred to both of them.

Utility functions can have the same forms as production functions: polynomials, Cobb-Douglas functions \(U = Ax^{\alpha}y^{\beta}\), and functions with roots, logarithms or exponential functions.

Indifference curves

All the baskets that give the same utility form an indifference curve. It is a level curve of the utility function:

\[ U(x;\,y) = C \]

Along one indifference curve the utility does not change. A curve further from the origin corresponds to larger amounts of both goods and to a higher utility.

Marginal utility

The first order partial derivatives of the utility function are the marginal utilities:

\[ MU_x = U'_x;\qquad MU_y = U'_y \]

The marginal utility of the first good, \(MU_x\), shows how the utility changes when the consumption of the first good increases by one unit and the amount of the second good does not change. The marginal utility of the second good, \(MU_y\), is interpreted in the same way. The interpretation is part of the answer:

If the consumption of the first good increases by one unit from \(x_0\), while the amount of the second good stays \(y_0\), the utility will change by approximately \(MU_x(x_0;\,y_0)\) units.
If the consumption of the second good increases by one unit from \(y_0\), while the amount of the first good stays \(x_0\), the utility will change by approximately \(MU_y(x_0;\,y_0)\) units.

Marginal utility is usually positive, because more of a good brings more satisfaction. As consumption grows, marginal utility usually decreases: the first cup of coffee of the day brings more satisfaction than the fifth.

The marginal rate of substitution

Let the consumer have the basket \((x_0;\,y_0)\) on an indifference curve. Along this curve the total utility does not change.

  • If the consumer gets one additional unit of the first good, the utility increases by approximately \(MU_x\).
  • To keep the utility unchanged, some of the second good has to be given up. When one unit of it is given up, the utility changes by approximately \(-MU_y\).
  • Denote the change in the amount of the second good by \(\Delta y\). The utility then changes by approximately \(MU_y\cdot\Delta y\): giving up one unit means \(\Delta y = -1\), and the corresponding change of the utility is \(-MU_y\).
  • For the decrease of the utility to offset the increase, the sum of the two changes is set equal to zero:
\[ MU_x\cdot 1 + MU_y\cdot\Delta y = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \Delta y = -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} MU_x\cdot 1 + MU_y\cdot\Delta y = 0 \\[4pt] \Downarrow \\[4pt] \Delta y = -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y} \end{gathered} \]

This change in the amount of the second good, which keeps the same utility when the amount of the first good increases by one unit, is called the marginal rate of substitution:

\[ MRS = -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = -\frac{U'_x}{U'_y} \]

The marginal rate of substitution measures the rate at which the consumer is willing to substitute one good for the other so that the utility does not change. In the figure the amount of the first good increases by one unit, and the amount of the second good decreases by approximately \(|MRS|\) units, with the same utility. The MRS is negative because the amount of the second good decreases. This is also why the indifference curve slopes downwards.

One additional unit of the first good is compensated by a change in the amount of the second good approximately equal to the MRS, and the consumer stays on the same indifference curve. For the function \(U = xy\) the marginal utilities are \(MU_x = y\) and \(MU_y = x\), so \(MRS = -\frac{y}{x}\).

Remark. In some textbooks the marginal rate of substitution is defined as the absolute value \(MRS = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y}\), i.e. a positive number. Both versions describe the same exchange; the minus sign only shows that the amount of the second good decreases.

The interpretation

If \(MRS(x_0;\,y_0) = -m\), then with the basket \((x_0;\,y_0)\) the consumer is willing to give up approximately \(m\) units of the second good in exchange for one additional unit of the first good, so that the utility does not change.

The MRS usually depends on the basket. A consumer who already has a lot of the first good is willing to give up only a small amount of the second good for one more unit of it. So \(|MRS|\) decreases as \(x\) increases – this can also be seen in the figure by moving basket A to the right with the slider.

When the first good does not increase by one unit

If the amount of the first good changes by \(\Delta x\) units, for example decreases by one unit (\(\Delta x = -1\)), the change in the amount of the second good that keeps the same utility is approximately

\[ \Delta y \approx MRS\cdot\Delta x \]

When \(\Delta x = -1\), \(\Delta y\) is positive: the loss is compensated by an additional amount of the second good (see Example 4).

How to solve

A problem may ask for the MRS and its interpretation directly. The same thing may also be asked in other words: how much of one good the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of another good. Then the good whose amount changes by one unit is the first variable \(x\), and the good that compensates this change is the second variable \(y\).

  1. Write the function. Decide which good changes by one unit and which one compensates this change.
  2. Find the two partial derivatives – the marginal utilities.
  3. Write the ratio with the minus sign: \(MRS = -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y}\). Common factors cancel, so it is often easier to simplify the fraction before substituting the numbers.
  4. Substitute the given basket. If the amount of the first good changes by \(\Delta x\) units, calculate \(\Delta y \approx MRS\cdot\Delta x\).
  5. Interpret the result with the wording of the interpretation.

The same steps are used for the marginal rate of technical substitution, which is discussed in the next section.

!Common mistakes
  • The variables mixed up. The good that changes by one unit is the first variable \(x\), and the good that compensates is the second one, \(y\). This decides which marginal utility is written in the numerator.
  • The ratio turned upside down. \(MRS = -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y}\): the numerator is the marginal utility of the good that changes by one unit.
  • A negative amount given in the interpretation. When \(MRS = -m\), the consumer is willing to give up approximately \(m\) units of the second good.
  • The direction of the change ignored. When the amount of the first good decreases (\(\Delta x = -1\)), the compensating change \(\Delta y \approx MRS\cdot\Delta x\) is positive – more of the second good is needed.
  • The other good not mentioned in the interpretation of a marginal utility. Say that the amount of the other good does not change.

Marginal utilities

Example 1

Books and concerts

Problem A student’s yearly utility from reading \(x\) books and going to \(y\) concerts is
\[ U(x;\,y) = 20x + 30y - x^2 - 2y^2 + xy \]
\[ \begin{aligned} U(x;\,y) &= 20x + 30y \\ &\phantom{=}\; {}- x^2 - 2y^2 + xy \end{aligned} \]
The student reads 5 books and goes to 4 concerts a year. Find the marginal utilities of books and of concerts. Interpret the results.

1Marginal utilities

With respect to \(x\) the terms \(30y\) and \(2y^2\) are constants, and with respect to \(y\) the terms \(20x\) and \(x^2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= 20 - 2x + y \\ MU_y &= 30 - 4y + x \end{aligned} \]

2The values

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x(5;\,4) &= 20 - 10 + 4 = 14 \\ MU_y(5;\,4) &= 30 - 16 + 5 = 19 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

If the number of books increases by one from 5, while the number of concerts stays 4, the utility will increase by approximately 14 units. If the number of concerts increases by one from 4, while the number of books stays 5, the utility will increase by approximately 19 units.

Example 2

Coffee and tea

Problem A consumer’s weekly utility from \(x\) cups of coffee and \(y\) cups of tea is \(U(x;\,y) = 8\sqrt{x} + 12\sqrt{y}\). Find the marginal utilities of coffee and of tea when the consumer drinks 16 cups of coffee and 9 cups of tea a week. Interpret the results.

1Marginal utilities

By the rule \(\big(\sqrt{x}\big)'_x = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= 8\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{x}} \\[6pt] MU_y &= 12\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{y}} \end{aligned} \]

2The values

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x(16;\,9) &= \frac{4}{4} = 1 \\[6pt] MU_y(16;\,9) &= \frac{6}{3} = 2 \end{aligned} \]
Answer

If the consumption of coffee increases by one cup from 16, while the consumption of tea stays 9 cups, the utility will increase by approximately 1 unit. If the consumption of tea increases by one cup from 9, while the consumption of coffee stays 16 cups, the utility will increase by approximately 2 units.

The marginal rate of substitution

Example 3

Films and series

Problem A viewer’s weekly utility from watching films for \(x\) hours and series for \(y\) hours is \(U(x;\,y) = x^{0.6}y^{0.4}\). Find the marginal rate of substitution when the viewer watches 8 hours of films and 12 hours of series a week. Interpret the result.

1Marginal utilities

This is a Cobb-Douglas utility function, and the power rule gives

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= 0.6x^{-0.4}y^{0.4} \\ MU_y &= 0.4x^{0.6}y^{-0.6} \end{aligned} \]

2MRS

Powers with the same base are divided by subtracting the exponents: \(\frac{x^{-0.4}}{x^{0.6}} = x^{-1}\) and \(\frac{y^{0.4}}{y^{-0.6}} = y^1\), so

\[ MRS = -\frac{0.6x^{-0.4}y^{0.4}}{0.4x^{0.6}y^{-0.6}} = -1.5\cdot\frac{y}{x} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} MRS &= -\frac{0.6x^{-0.4}y^{0.4}}{0.4x^{0.6}y^{-0.6}} \\[6pt] &= -1.5\cdot\frac{y}{x} \end{aligned} \]

For every Cobb-Douglas utility function \(U = Ax^{\alpha}y^{\beta}\) the same calculation gives \(MRS = -\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\frac{y}{x}\).

3The value

At the basket \((8;\,12)\):

\[ MRS(8;\,12) = -1.5\cdot\frac{12}{8} = -2.25 \]
Answer

The MRS is \(-2.25\): with 8 hours of films and 12 hours of series a week, the viewer is willing to give up approximately 2.25 hours of series in exchange for one additional hour of films, so that the utility does not change.

Example 4

Gym and swimming pool

Problem A consumer’s monthly utility from \(x\) visits to a gym and \(y\) visits to a swimming pool is \(U(x;\,y) = (x + 4)(y + 1)\). She plans 8 visits to the gym and 17 visits to the swimming pool this month. The gym will be closed for one day, so she can make one gym visit fewer. How many additional visits to the swimming pool would keep her utility unchanged?

1What is asked

The gym visits change by one unit and the swimming pool visits compensate this change. So \(x\) is the number of gym visits and \(y\) is the number of swimming pool visits. One gym visit fewer means \(\Delta x = -1\).

2Marginal utilities

With respect to \(x\) the bracket \((y + 1)\) is a constant multiplier, and the derivative of \(x + 4\) is 1. With respect to \(y\) the roles of the variable and the constant change:

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= y + 1; &\qquad MU_x(8;\,17) &= 18 \\ MU_y &= x + 4; &\qquad MU_y(8;\,17) &= 12 \end{aligned} \]

3The MRS and the change

\[ \begin{aligned} MRS &= -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = -\frac{18}{12} = -1.5 \\[6pt] \Delta y &\approx MRS\cdot\Delta x = (-1.5)\cdot(-1) = 1.5 \end{aligned} \]

One gym visit fewer lowers the utility by approximately 18 units, and an additional visit to the swimming pool raises it by approximately 12 units. So the loss is compensated by 1.5 visits.

Answer

Approximately 1.5 additional visits to the swimming pool would keep her utility unchanged.

Example 5

Fruit and juice

Problem A consumer’s monthly utility from \(x\) kilograms of fresh fruit and \(y\) litres of juice is \[ U(x;\,y) = 20\ln x + 30\ln y \] The consumer buys 6 kilograms of fruit and 18 litres of juice a month. How many litres of juice is the consumer willing to give up for one more kilogram of fruit?

1Marginal utilities

The rule \(\big(\ln x\big)'_x = \frac{1}{x}\) gives

\[ MU_x = \frac{20}{x};\qquad MU_y = \frac{30}{y} \]

2MRS

Dividing by a fraction is multiplying by the inverted fraction:

\[ MRS = -\frac{20}{x}:\frac{30}{y} = -\frac{20}{x}\cdot\frac{y}{30} = -\frac{2y}{3x} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} MRS &= -\frac{20}{x}:\frac{30}{y} \\[6pt] &= -\frac{20}{x}\cdot\frac{y}{30} = -\frac{2y}{3x} \end{aligned} \]
\[ MRS(6;\,18) = -\frac{2\cdot 18}{3\cdot 6} = -2 \]

3The change

One more kilogram of fruit means \(\Delta x = 1\), so \(\Delta y \approx MRS\cdot 1 = -2\): the amount of juice decreases by approximately 2 litres.

Answer

The consumer is willing to give up approximately 2 litres of juice for one more kilogram of fruit.

Example 6

Holidays at the seaside and in the mountains

Problem A traveller’s yearly utility from \(x\) holiday days at the seaside and \(y\) holiday days in the mountains is \[ U(x;\,y) = 60 - 24e^{-0.5x} - 20e^{-0.2y} \] a) Find and interpret the marginal rate of substitution for 4 days at the seaside and 10 days in the mountains.
b) Find and interpret the marginal rate of substitution for 6 days at the seaside and 10 days in the mountains.

1Marginal utilities

Each exponential term is a composite function. The inner function \(-0.5x\) has the derivative \(-0.5\), and \(-0.2y\) has the derivative \(-0.2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= -24e^{-0.5x}\cdot(-0.5) = 12e^{-0.5x} \\ MU_y &= -20e^{-0.2y}\cdot(-0.2) = 4e^{-0.2y} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= -24e^{-0.5x}\cdot(-0.5) = 12e^{-0.5x} \\ MU_y &= -20e^{-0.2y}\cdot(-0.2) = 4e^{-0.2y} \end{aligned} \]

2MRS

Powers with the same base are divided by subtracting the exponents:

\[ MRS = -\frac{12e^{-0.5x}}{4e^{-0.2y}} = -3e^{-0.5x + 0.2y} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} MRS &= -\frac{12e^{-0.5x}}{4e^{-0.2y}} \\[6pt] &= -3e^{-0.5x + 0.2y} \end{aligned} \]

3a) The basket \((4;\,10)\)

At the point \((4;\,10)\) the exponent is \(-2 + 2 = 0\):

\[ MRS(4;\,10) = -3e^0 = -3 \]

4b) The basket \((6;\,10)\)

At the point \((6;\,10)\) the exponent is \(-3 + 2 = -1\):

\[ MRS(6;\,10) = -3e^{-1} \approx -1.10 \]

With more days at the seaside, the traveller is willing to give up fewer days in the mountains for one more day at the seaside.

Answer

a) The MRS is \(-3\): with 4 days at the seaside and 10 days in the mountains, the traveller is willing to give up approximately 3 days in the mountains in exchange for one additional day at the seaside, so that the utility does not change.

b) The MRS is approximately \(-1.10\): with 6 days at the seaside and 10 days in the mountains, the traveller is willing to give up approximately 1.10 days in the mountains in exchange for one more day at the seaside.

Practice problems

Write the solutions out as in the examples: find the marginal utilities, write the MRS and simplify it, substitute the given basket and interpret the result. The answer gives the intermediate results and the interpretation – use them to check your work.

A student’s monthly utility from \(x\) takeaway pizzas and \(y\) sets of sushi is

\[ U(x;\,y) = x^{0.25}y^{0.75} \]

Find the marginal rate of substitution when the student buys 6 pizzas and 9 sets of sushi a month. Interpret the result.

The marginal utilities:

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= 0.25x^{-0.75}y^{0.75} \\ MU_y &= 0.75x^{0.25}y^{-0.25} \end{aligned} \]

The MRS:

\[ MRS = -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = -\frac{0.25x^{-0.75}y^{0.75}}{0.75x^{0.25}y^{-0.25}} = -\frac{y}{3x} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} MRS &= -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = -\frac{0.25x^{-0.75}y^{0.75}}{0.75x^{0.25}y^{-0.25}} \\[6pt] &= -\frac{y}{3x} \end{aligned} \]
\[ MRS(6;\,9) = -\frac{9}{18} = -0.5 \]

Answer. The MRS is \(-0.5\): with 6 pizzas and 9 sets of sushi a month, the student is willing to give up approximately 0.5 set of sushi in exchange for one additional pizza, so that the utility does not change.

A consumer’s yearly utility from \(x\) visits to the theatre and \(y\) football matches is

\[ U(x;\,y) = 2xy + 3x + 4y \]

This year the consumer plans 8 visits to the theatre and 6 football matches.

a) Find the marginal utilities and interpret the results.

b) The consumer has to give up one visit to the theatre. Approximately how many additional football matches would keep the utility unchanged?

The marginal utilities:

\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= 2y + 3; &\qquad MU_x(8;\,6) &= 15 \\ MU_y &= 2x + 4; &\qquad MU_y(8;\,6) &= 20 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} MU_x &= 2y + 3 \\ MU_y &= 2x + 4 \\[6pt] MU_x(8;\,6) &= 15 \\ MU_y(8;\,6) &= 20 \end{aligned} \]

The MRS and the change:

\[ \begin{aligned} MRS &= -\frac{15}{20} = -0.75 \\[6pt] \Delta y &\approx MRS\cdot\Delta x = (-0.75)\cdot(-1) = 0.75 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} MRS &= -\frac{15}{20} = -0.75 \\[6pt] \Delta y &\approx MRS\cdot\Delta x \\ &= (-0.75)\cdot(-1) = 0.75 \end{aligned} \]

Answer. a) If the number of theatre visits increases by one from 8, while the number of matches stays 6, the utility will increase by approximately 15 units. If the number of matches increases by one from 6, while the number of theatre visits stays 8, the utility will increase by approximately 20 units. b) Approximately 0.75 additional football match.
